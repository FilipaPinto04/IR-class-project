RESUMO
Contexto: A etiologia da malária envolve a infiltração de parasitas Plasmodium no corpo humano,
tipicamente transmitida através da picada de mosquitos Anopheles fêmeas. Esta doença continua a ser um
desafio significativo para a saúde global devido ao seu severo impacto e à transmissão eficiente entre
humanos e mosquitos. Agravando o problema está o surgimento de resistência aos tratamentos de primeira
linha atuais, como as terapias combinadas à base de artemisinina (ACTs), particularmente para casos não
complicados de malária por P. falciparum. Abordar este desafio exige o desenvolvimento de fármacos
inovadores e potentes. As abordagens terapêuticas futuras são concebidas para atacar não apenas o
estágio sintomático, mas também o estágio de gametócitos que é menos metabolicamente ativo e muitas
vezes negligenciado, crucial para interromper a transmissão de humano para mosquito. No entanto, um
obstáculo significativo nesta busca é a escassez de medicamentos adaptados a este estágio de gametócitos
não sintomáticos, em grande parte devido a limitações na produção em massa e na quantificação precisa,
que dificultam os esforços de triagem de fármacos em grande escala.
Objetivos: Construir uma linhagem de parasitas que pode ser geneticamente induzida para sofrer
gametocitogénese. Além disso, pretendemos melhorar ainda mais esta linha incorporando fluorescência
para deteção simplificada de gametócitos.
Metodologia: Especificamente, empregamos metodologias de edição de genoma de parasitas, incluindo o
sistema CRISPR-Cas9, o sistema de integrase Bxb1 e a integração vinculada à seleção (SLI), para construir
uma linhagem de parasitas com relatório usando dois promotores específicos de estágio (para assexual e
gametócito) a montante de proteínas fluorescentes distintas e um sistema induzível para promover a
conversão sexual. Isso facilitará a deteção e quantificação precisas dos dois estágios através de citometria
de fluxo ou outras leituras baseadas em imagem.
Resultados: Começando com parasitas assexuais, um plasmídeo pSLI-HP1-GFP-glmS foi desenhado para
marcar os parasitas assexuais com GFP através de integração vinculada à seleção, juntamente com um
sistema glmS induzível para a redução condicional da proteína de heterocromatina 1 (HP1), o regulador do
fator de transcrição AP2-G responsável pela gametocitogénese. Para gerar a linha repórter de gametócitos,
tentamos introduzir uma fusão com uma proteína fluorescente vermelha-luciferase (EmiRFP670Luc) sob
um promotor específico de gametócitos (pfs16) usando o sistema CRISPR-Cas9 e/ou o sistema de
recombinase attP-attB. Construímos um plasmídeo CRISPR-Cas9 personalizado para inserir uma região
attB na estirpe P. falciparum NF54, mas não conseguimos integrar a cassete repórter EmiRFP670Luc
através do sistema de integrase Bxb1. Posteriormente, tentamos a expressão episomal de EmiRFP670Luc
sob o promotor pfs16, mas não obtivemos parasitas editados. São necessárias investigações adicionais
para alcançar o sucesso neste aspeto.
Conclusão: Existe a necessidade urgente de novos fármacos antimaláricos que visem tanto os estágios
sintomáticos como os não sintomáticos do ciclo de vida do parasita para interromper eficazmente a
transmissão de humano para mosquito. Apesar dos desafios encontrados na construção de uma linha
repórter de gametócitos, nossa abordagem inovadora usando metodologias de edição de genoma de
parasitas lançou as bases para a deteção e quantificação precisas de estágios assexuais e gametócitos.
Embora as tentativas iniciais de introduzir a cassete repórter EmiRFP670Luc não tenham sido bem-
sucedidas, são necessárias investigações adicionais para superar estes obstáculos e avançar no
desenvolvimento de terapias eficazes contra a malária.
Palavras – Chave: Plasmodium falciparum; estratégias de edição genómica; gametócitos; drogas
antimaláricas.

ABSTRACT
Background: The etiology of malaria involves the infiltration of Plasmodium parasites into the human body,
typically transmitted through the bite of female Anopheles mosquitoes. This disease remains a significant
global health challenge due to its severe impact and efficient transmission between humans and
mosquitoes. In addition, we must consider the emergence of resistance to current first-line treatments, such
as artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), particularly for uncomplicated cases of P. falciparum
malaria. Addressing this challenge necessitates the development of innovative and potent drugs. Future
therapeutic approaches are envisioned to target not only the symptomatic stage but also the less
metabolically active and often neglected gametocyte stage, which is essential for the interruption of the
human-to-mosquito transmission. However, a significant obstacle in this pursuit is the scarcity of drugs
tailored to this non-symptomatic gametocyte stage, largely due to limitations in mass production and
accurate quantification, which hinder high-throughput drug screening efforts.
Objectives: Engineer a gametocyte reporter line that can be genetically induced for gametocytogenesis.
Moreover, we aim to further improve this line by incorporating fluorescence for streamlined detection of
gametocytes.
Methodology: Specifically, we employed parasite genome editing methodologies, including the CRISPR-Cas9
system, Bxb1 integrase system, and selection-linked integration (SLI), to build a reporter parasite line using
two stage-specific promoters (for asexual and gametocyte) upstream of distinct fluorescent proteins and an
inducible system to promote gametocyte commitment. This will facilitate accurate detection and
quantification of the two stages through flow cytometry or other imaging-based readouts.
Results: Beginning with asexual parasites, a customized pSLI-HP1-GFP-glmS plasmid was engineered to
label asexual parasites with GFP through selection-linked integration, along with an inducible glmS system
to conditional knockdown of Heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1), the regulator of the AP2-G transcription factor
responsible for gametocytogenesis. To generate a gametocyte reporter line, we attempted to introduce a
red fluorescent protein-luciferase fusion (EmiRFP670Luc) under a gametocyte-specific promoter (pfs16)
using the CRISPR-Cas9 system and/or attP-attB recombinase system. We constructed a customized
CRISPR-Cas9 plasmid to insert an attB region into the P. falciparum NF54 strain but were not successful in
integrating the EmiRFP670Luc reporter cassette through the Bxb1 integrase system. Subsequently, we
attempted the episomal expression of EmiRFP670Luc under the pfs16 promoter but failed to obtain edited
parasites. Further investigations are warranted to succeed in this endeavour.
Conclusion: Our study underscores the urgent need for novel antimalarial drugs targeting both symptomatic
and non-symptomatic stages of the parasite life cycle to effectively interrupt human-to-mosquito
transmission. Despite encountering challenges in constructing a gametocyte reporter line, our innovative
approach - using parasite genome editing - methodologies has laid the groundwork for accurate detection
and quantification of asexual and gametocyte stages. While initial attempts to introduce the EmiRFP670Luc
reporter cassette were unsuccessful, further investigations are warranted to overcome these obstacles and
advance toward developing effective therapies against malaria.
Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum; Gametocytes; genome – editing strategies; antimalarial drugs.

1.1. Malaria Burden
Malaria is a life-threatening illness caused by the transmission of Plasmodium parasites through
the bite of an infected female mosquito of the Anopheles genus [1]. According to the World Health
Organization (WHO), in 2022, there were an estimated 249 million cases of malaria across 84
malaria-endemic countries [2]. Malaria stands as one of the deadliest diseases globally, with an
estimated 608,000 deaths recorded in 2022 [2].
Figure 1 - Number of Indigenous Malaria Cases 2000 - 2022 according to the World Health Organization (WHO)
While the European region is free from indigenous cases of endemic malaria, as illustrated in
Figure 1, approximately 85 countries across Africa, Asia, and the Americas still face the threat of
this deadly disease [2]. Recent data from the European Cases 2020 report (Figure 2) indicates five
cases acquired in France and Greece [3]. Moreover, there are emerging challenges in the
prevention, control, and elimination of malaria. The WHO highlights the growing concern about
antimalarial resistance and its impact on the effectiveness of treatment, which will be further
addressed in this thesis. Additionally, a significant issue to be addressed is that common
antimalarial drugs do not target vector transmission [4]. Therefore, this work focuses on
addressing vector transmission, to combat malaria effectively.

Malaria disease occurs due to the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitos with Plasmodium
parasites in the host skin [1]. Naturally, there are approximately 186 known species of
Plasmodium. Five species are known to infect humans (Figure 3), namely Plasmodium falciparum,
Plasmodium ovale, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium malariae, and P. knowlesi [4]. Because

1.2. Plasmodium falciparum Life Cycle
1.2.1. Human Host: Liver Stages
When a female Anopheles mosquito takes a blood meal, it injects sporozoites into the human
host's skin [9]. Once the sporozoites are in the skin, they can enter the bloodstream and migrate
to the liver sinusoids through gliding motility [10], [11], [12]. Within the liver sinusoids, they
traverse the inner membrane of blood vessels to reach the liver. Once in the liver, the sporozoites
invade Kupffer cells and hepatocytes before initiating mass production of merozoites. The liver
stage commences when sporozoites infect hepatocytes and form parasitophorous vacuoles, thus
transforming into exoerythrocytic forms (EEFs) [13]. These EEFs undergo multiplication, each
yielding thousands of merozoites. The Hepatocyte Stage marks the initial phase in the human host,
typically lasting 7 to 14 days after infection, during which patients remain clinically
asymptomatic[14].
1.2.2. Human Host: Red Blood Cells Stage Exo – erythrocytic cycle
In section 1.2.1, the liver merozoites undergo uncontrolled asexual replication, leading to their
release into the bloodstream and subsequent invasion of red blood cells (RBCs) [12]. This outburst
of merozoites into the bloodstream marks the initiation of the erythrocytic cycle [10]. Within this
cycle, merozoites differentiate into rings, trophozoites, and schizonts, culminating in the release of
16 to 32 new merozoites during theegress process known as exo-erythrocytic schizogony, which
typically occurs 48 hours post-invasion [11], [12]. Moreover, the asexual replication and
subsequent infection of RBCs are associated with the onset of clinical symptoms [13], [14].
1.2.3. Human Host: Red Blood Cells Stage: Intraerythrocytic cycle
Merozoites infect erythrocytes through penetration. Furthermore, these parasites undergo
asexual replication, giving rise to rings, immature trophozoites, mature trophozoites, and schizonts,
with cyclic transformations every 48 hours [9]. Simultaneously, a small number of immature
trophozoites may undergo a stress stimulus and initiate the sexual pathway, becoming sexual
parasites, and ultimately developing into gametocytes [12], [13]. While gametocytes themselves
may not correspond to any clinical symptom’s manifestation, they facilitate disease transmission
by serving as carriers and reservoirs [14]. These gametocytes undergo mitosis, resulting in the
formation of haploid daughter cells (n) across five stages [15], [16]. Additionally, sexual parasites
can differentiate into males (microgametocytes) and females (macrogametocytes), thus undergoing
gametocytogenesis from stage IV to V [17], [18]. To produce eight flagellated microgametes, their
4

genome undertakes three replications. The release of mature males is referred to as exflagellation
[19].
1.2.4. Mosquito Host: Sporogonic Development
To conclude the life cycle of Plasmodium falciparum (Figure 4), a female mosquito from the
Anopheles genus feeds on an infected human, acquiring gametocytes [17]. Moreover, the fusion
of female and male gametocytes is essential for the formation of a diploid zygote (2n) [18], [19].
Following fusion, sporogonic development occurs on the mosquito midgut epithelium, leading to
the mass production of haploid sporozoites[19]. The whole parasite life cycle is represented in
Figure 4.

1.3. Beginning of the sexual pathway
1.3.1. Gametocyte Morphology
Gametocytes represent the sexual form of Plasmodium falciparum parasites that develop inside
human red blood cells. There are five developmental stages leading to fully mature and functional
gametocytes (Figure 5). At stage I, gametocytes resemble young trophozoites with a round shape
[20], [21]. During stage II, the gametocytes initially enlarge in their round shape and then elongate
into the final D-shape. This sexual stage exhibits a pigmented and granular distribution in the food
vacuoles and takes 1 day to mature into the next stage [20], [21]. Moving to stage III, gametocytes
5

assume a distorted oval morphology within the red blood cell, which requires 3 days to mature up
to stage IV. Stage IV features an elongated, elegant structure with pointed tips, allowing for the
distinction between females and males. This stage also takes 3 days to mature to stage V. In stage
IV gametocytes, the pigments in the food vacuoles are more dispersed in males than in females
(appearing thicker)[22]. Finally, stage V presents a falciform or crescent shape, with males
exhibiting pale and thicker cytoplasm, and females having more blue cytoplasm under Giemsa
staining. Stage V gametocytes can be observed in peripheral circulation after 10-12 days of
maturation[23], [24]. While stages I to IV are typically detected in the bone marrow, stage V is
primarily found in peripheral circulation [22].
Figure 5 - Plasmodium falciparum Gametocytes Morphology developmental stages. Made in Biorender.com
1.3.2. Gametocyte Formation
The precise moment of the first appearance of gametocytes is yet unknown, although various
theories have been proposed over time. In 1979, Carter and Miller exposed three different possible
theories to explain this phenomenon [25]. Model I (Figure 6.1) suggest that the commitment
towards becoming gametocytes occurs either during the merozoite stage or in the ring stage.
According to this model, the decision to become either asexual parasites or gametocytes depends
on a stress stimulus, indicating that the ring stage is neutral within this context[25]. In contrast,
Model II (Figure 6.2) proposes that it occurs during the asexual phase while only expressing it in
the subsequent generation [25]. However, in this scenario, all progenies will either exclusively
consist of gametocytes or asexual parasites. The last hypothesis, Model III (Figure 6.3), resembles
Model II but differs from it in its progeny composition. In this case, the same culture contains both
gametocytes and asexual parasites from the same progeny [25]. These theories attempt to shed
6

light on the enigmatic process of gametocyte formation, highlighting the complexities involved in
Plasmodium parasite sexual development.

can occur in red blood cells, where a committed post-invasion single merozoite will differentiate
into gametocytes or asexual parasites [26], [27], [28].

1.4. The Sexual Switch
1.4.1. AP2-G – The Sexual Master Key Regulator
The development of Plasmodium parasite gametocytes is guided by specific transcription factors,
notably ApiAP2 (Apicomplexan AP2)[30]. ApiAP2, a DNA-binding protein family, is recognized as
the orchestrator of all developmental transitions in Plasmodium. Among them, PfAP2-G serves as
the master regulator responsible for the sexual switch and sexual commitment [31], [32], [33].
Downstream of the key master PfAP2-G, several transcription factors have been identified, including
AP2-G2[34], AP2-G3 [35], [36], [37], and AP2-G5[29]. AP2-G2 plays a critical role in gametocyte
maturation, as it is expressed from very early trophozoite stages up to gametocyte stage V [34].
On the other hand, AP2-G3, only recently discovered , facilitates the transmission of cytoplasmic
signals to the nucleus, resulting in increased AP2-G transcription levels, with mild expression
observed in gametocyte stages II-IV [36]. In contrast, AP2-G5 binds upstream of AP2-G, repressing
the expression of early gametocyte genes and consequently inhibiting gametocytogenesis [29].
8

These findings shed light on the intricate regulatory mechanisms underlying gametocyte
development, emphasizing the pivotal role of transcription factors in this process (Figure 8).
Figure 8 - Transcription Factor involved with sexual cascade to generate gametocytes.
The regulation of AP2-G genes is made by the presence of HP1 (Heterochromatic protein
1)[38]. In addition, HP1 has three different domains (Figure 9): the N-terminus contains a
conserved chromodomain (CD), that is responsible for the H3K9me3 binding; the C-terminus
includes a conserved chromoshadow domain (CSD) [39] that allows HP1’s dimerization and its
interaction with other chromatin motifs; and a third variable domain (hinge) which separates CD
and CSD and interacts with the H1 histone, DNA or RNA[40]. Thus, HP1 expression occurs in all
chromosomes in subtelomeric regions.

located near the internal part of H3K9me3/HP1 chromosomes – demarcated heterochromatic
islands [41], [42], thus being crucial for asexual proliferation and the silencing of blood-stage genes
[43], [44].

1.4.2. The interplay between HP1 and GDV1
The Gametocyte Development Protein 1 (GDV1) is a perinuclear protein found in Plasmodium
falciparum, holding a gametocytogenesis function that was reported for the first time in 2012 [45].
Moreover, the overexpression of GDV1 has been shown to enhance gametocytes is production in
parasites lacking gdv1, as observed in 3D7 strains [45]. Further studies [46] have reinforced the
role of GDV1 in the regulation of molecular mechanisms underlying gametocytogenesis. GDV1 has
been demonstrated to induce a sexual commitment by silencing HP1, a natural repressor of
gametocytogenesis, through an epigenetic control strategy which involves GDV1-mediated
heterochromatin impairment in Plasmodium falciparum (Figure 10)[46].

1.4.3. Gametocyte Development Genes
a) Gametocyte Early Genes
Although there is scarce knowledge of the molecular pathways and genes involved in
gametogenesis, there are some molecular markers which help us track gametocytes, such as:
HomeoDomain – like protein (HDP1) is involved in the falciform gametocytes [49]; H3K36me2
and 3 mark theline between early and intermediate gametocyte development; GEXP2 signal is
relevant withinthe early sexual ring stage; pfg14-744 in the erythrocyte cytoplasm when
gametocytes startdeveloping [50]; pfg14-748, that is found near the parasitophorous vesicle [50],
and Plasmodium falciparum sexual stage-specific protein 16 (Pfs16), which represents the earliest
gametocyte marker [51].
b) Gametocyte Late Genes
There are only few gametocyte-related late genes known to date, such as Pfs28. Pfs28 is a sexual
late marker expressed in stages IV and V of gametocytes [52]. It is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-
anchored protein, important for the impairment of gametes in the mosquito midgut [53]. Moreover,
ithas been involved in the production of an antibody – the Pfs28/ Pfs25 vaccine, employed to
block vector transmission [54]. Furthermore, Pfs25 has a similar function in relation to ookinetes’
survival and parasite’s survival in the mosquito midgut [55].
c) Pfs16: The Earliest Gametocyte Marker
The Plasmodium falciparum sexual stage-specific protein 16 (Pfs16) is an early gene marker for
gametocytes [56]. Pfs16 is expressed 35 hours post-merozoite invasion in the parasitophorous
vacuole membrane in the parasite [57]. Pfs16’s expression is continuous during gametocytes stage
I to V [58], making it a good candidate to report the presence of gametocytes and all sexual
development.
In early 2023, a new function of Psf16 was discovered, specifically with a silencing role in the
mosquito’s immune system and promoting vector transmission [59].
d) Sex Patterns Genes Differences
Several studies have identified potential differences in commitment between males and females
regarding sex differentiation. In males (producing microgametes), specific markers have been
identified, such as a mutation on chromosome 12 known as PfMAP-2[60], essential for
microgametocyte development and exflagellation. Additionally, the nuclear microtubule-associated
End Binding protein 1 (PfEB1) plays a crucial role in male fertility [61]. Chromatin patterns
11

indicative of male differentiation are influenced by gametocyte sucrose nonfermentable 2 (gSNF2)
during early gametocyte stages[62]. On the other hand, unique features in females include the
transcription factor AP2-O3, which acts as a repressor of female gametocyte maturation, leading
to the production of immature female gametocytes. Moreover, high expression levels of histones
H2A.Z and H2B.Z have been observed in H3K9me3-associated heterochromatin in female
gametocytes[63].
Despite the scarce knowledge about sex differentiation and stage development in gametocytes,
in 2023 novel targets related to gametocytes and sex differentiation were discovered. However,
there is a need to further analyze and dive deeper in the parasite genome and proteomics in order
to discover novel genes to target the interruption of vector transmission using transmission-blocking
strategies. The drug screening differentiation between males and females is crucial to understand
the interruption of vector transmission as stated by natural law.
1.5. Strategies to induce Gametocytogenesis in vitro
1.5.1. Stress Protocol
To ensure optimal quantity and quality of gametocyte production, attention must be paid to four
key factors: parasite strain, serum, blood, and temperature (Figure 11)[64]. The choice of the
parasite strain is crucial, with a preference for a strain that yields a high number of gametocytes,
ideally from a fresh culture[65]. Over time, parasite strains may lose their ability to generate new
gametocytes with each multiplication, making it essential to use fresh cultures [66]. The
Plasmodium falciparum NF54 strain represents an excellent candidate within this context, allowing
a high-scale production and maintenance of gametocytes for over two months [64]. Serum
selection is blood-dependent and highly variable for optimal gametocyte production [67], [68].
Therefore, blood must be compatible with the serum, with O+ and A+ blood types being commonly
utilized; the use of fresh blood is advised [64]. Temperature plays a critical role in determining
gametocyte appearance, with cultures ideally being maintained at 37°C [64], [69]. It's important
to note that serum preparation, sterilization, parasite strain selection, timing and culture volumes,
represent important steps, which protocols should be optimized according to each laboratory
condition setting [64], [70].
12

Figure 11 - The four key factors to produce gametocytes: Blood type, blood-serum dependent, parasite strain, and
temperature. Made in Biorender.com
According to evolution, it was suggested that Plasmodium falciparum parasites suffer a sexual
commitment when triggered by environmental stress. Since the 20th century, obtaining
gametocytes in vitro cultures in the laboratory has been achieved byinducing healthy stress into
Plasmodium falciparum parasites. This stress can be related to drug pressure such as the addition
of quinoline[71], to the increase of cAMP signaling [70], to differences in the medium conditions,
and to higher parasitaemia levels. Also, the addition of Lactic acid [72] and unpolyunsaturated fatty
acids [73] promotes gametocytogenesis.
The stress method is a commonly used approach to obtain viable gametocytes. However, working
with gametocyte cultures in vitro presents several challenges. Firstly, in continuous cultures,
parasites often lose their ability to generate new gametocytes over time. Additionally, gametocytes
take approximately 10 to 15 days to fully mature through the five stages of development [64].
Furthermore, the requirement for daily medium changes is an addition s to the labor-intensive
nature of the process. Finally, there is a risk of biased results, which further complicates the
experimental process. Overall, these challenges contribute to an expensive, labor-intensive, and
time-consuming method for producing gametocytes.
As postulated by Fivelman, gametocyte production is dependent on initial parasitaemia to find
the optimal percentage of medium change [74]. According to the well–known stress protocol, high
parasitemia not only hampers asexual development but also increases cell death. The addition of
N-Acetylglucosamine (GlcNac) is crucial for the clearance of the mixed cultures, where the effect is
upon trophozoites and schizonts [64], [74].
We aim to optimize the mass production of gametocytes through genetic–editing strategies, in
order to be able to detect and quantify them through optical readout for drug screening purposes.

1.5.2. Genome–editing strategies
Several gene-editing strategies have been employed to understand Plasmodium falciparum is
biology and molecular mechanisms, aiming at the identification of novel drug targets. These
strategies involve gene deletion or insertion techniques, capitalizing on natural repair mechanisms
within the organism.
Living organisms possess two main repair mechanisms: non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and
homology-directed repair (HDR), which are activated in response to nuclease-induced double-
strand breaks (DSBs) in the genome (Figure 12) [75]. NHEJ repairs breaks by directly attaching
DNA ends, often resulting in small insertions or deletions at the break site[76]. In some cases,
such as when DSB occurs between homologous repeat regions in the same direction, a deletion
of one repeat can occur, followed by break closure mediated by a single-strand annealing pathway
(SSA). Conversely, HDR utilizes an external DNA template with identical homology arms to integrate
transgenes into the flanking region of DSB [77]. It is noteworthy that transgene integration via HDR
repair is more prone to failure compared to gene disruption with NHEJ repair. Optimal efficiency
of site-specific integration with HDR repair requires a high rate of replication of homologous sister
chromatids cells, with only around 30% of cells achieving the desired integration [78].
Figure 12 – Illustration of organism repair mechanisms: Homology-directed – Repair (HDR) and Non-Homologous
end-joining (NHEJ).
Plasmodium lacks the NHEJ machinery present in other organisms, relying instead on alternative
end-joining pathways during the G1 phase of mitosis and homologous recombination during phases
14

S, G2, and M when asynchronous mitosis and sister chromatids are present [79]. However, this
parasite uses A-T rich DNA templates for HDR cloning posing a challenge in fragment stability and
maintenance in Escherichia coli [80], [81].
Genetic engineering techniques such as CRISPR-Cas9[82], attP-attB site-specific recombination
[83], and Zinc-Finger Nucleases (ZFNs) [84] have been extensively employed for transgene
integration in Plasmodium falciparum’s genome. Within this context, the selection-linked integration
(SLI) and the riboswitch-inducible system (glmS) [85] are two systems that will be explored further
in this thesis.
a) CRISPR – Cas9 system
The Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR – Cas9) system is an
adaptive immune system found in prokaryotes, in which specific RNAs guide endonucleases
towards target genes[86]. The Cas9 endonuclease protein, derived from Streptococcus
thermophilus, contains a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) next to the target DNA sequence,
crucial for Cas9 binding. Spacers are transcribed into CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs) and trans-activating
CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA), forming a duplex that guides Cas9 to the target DNA sequence [87].
Single guide RNA directs Cas9 to generate a double-strand break (DSB) in the genome, with each
crRNA unit containing a 20-nucleotide guide sequence (Figure 13). This system has been utilized
to produce inducible gametocyte lines and transgenic reporter parasite lines. For instance, Boltryk's
research focused on engineering a reporter line to mark GDV1 via the CRISPR-Cas9 system, and
successfully transmitting mutant parasites to the mosquito and liver stage [88]. Another study
achieved conditional expression upstream of PfAP2-G to enhance sexual conversion [89]. CRISPR-
Cas9 has proven to be a highly successful gene editing technique in Plasmodium falciparum’s
genome using small insertions[90].

c) Zinc – Finger Nucleases (ZFNs)
The Zinc-Finger Nucleases (ZFNs) are protein domains capable of binding to specific sequences

The glmS ribozyme, found in Gram-positive bacteria, is unique and relies on a specific co-factor,

parasite’s culture medium [85]. The ribozyme is positioned near the 3' UTR after the coding region,
causing the mRNA to self-cleave when glucosamine binds to the ribozyme, thereby leading to the
knockdown of protein expression (Figure 16)[85].

1.6. Quantification of Gametocytes
1.6.1. Classical Method
Since the primordial ages, malaria parasites have been analyzed with Light Microscopy (LM).
The LM method is used for counting the differential stages and parasitemia, and is also accounted
by WHO to verify all suspected cases of malaria [100]. Although this method is available in almost
all laboratories, it requires a well-trained microscopist. Moreover, LM, allows low cost in diagnosis,
standard sensitivity for clinical examination, differentiation between malaria species and stages,
and the parasite density countdown [101]. Thin blood smears with Giemsa staining are used as
gloden standard to measure parasitaemia. It is important to note that LM is labor-intensive and
time-consuming, and it is not useful for high-throughput drug screenings.
1.6.2. Optical Readout Methods
Technological advancements are paramount for innovative research in Health Science
Investigations, particularly in molecular biology when considering molecular mechanisms and
proteins from various organisms. Luminescent and fluorescent proteins play vital roles in molecular
biology as markers for genes or proteins. Consequently, there is a demand for engineering new
and improved fluorescent and bioluminescent proteins with specific wavelengths across the
multicolor light spectrum, featuring diverse emission and excitation peaks.
19

In the past decade, numerous fluorochromes and fluorescent proteins have been developed for
transfection, including mCherry, mOrange, GFP, mNeptune, E2-Crimson, and enhanced
fluorescent proteins like EmiRFP670 [102]. For our focus, we consider Green Fluorescent Proteins
(GFP) [103] and EmiRFP670 [104]. While GFP, originating from the luminous jellyfish Aequorea
victoria, was the first to be utilized[105], there has been the development of a new monomeric set
of infra-red fluorescent proteins (iRFP). iRFPs are enhanced by supplementation with Biliverdin
(BV), exhibiting superior brightness compared to infra-red proteins (IFPs) in mammalian cells[102].
The optical window for RFP spans 650–900 nm and is characterized by low autofluorescence and
reduced cell toxicity. Recently, Matlashov and his team developed enhanced infra-red fluorescent
proteins, comprising a monomeric set with distinct spectra of light action, such as EmiRFP670 and
EmiRFP720 [104].
Bioluminescent proteins (Figure 18), sourced from various luminous organisms (e.g., Vibrio
harveyi, Photinus pyralis, Aequorea victoria), serve as reporters for gene expression and in vivo
imaging in eukaryotic cells[106]. Notably, Nanoluciferase boasts higher specificity, solubility,
thermostability, and slower decay of the reporter signal[107], [108].

1.7. Antimalarial Resistance and Effectiveness
Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) serve as the primary treatment for
uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria cases, as recommended by the World Health Organization
(WHO). ACTs involve combining an artemisinin-based compound, known for its rapid action, with
another drug that acts more slowly [111]. Artemisinin drugs play a critical role in reducing
parasitemia in the early stages of infection, typically acting within the first hour. The partner drug
is crucial for continued parasite clearance, preventing disease relapse in the same host [111]. This
combination is vital for achieving successful clinical and parasitological recovery and for preventing
the development of drug resistance [112].
1.7.1. Genes related to Antimalarial drug resistance
Since 2015, there has been a rise in antimalarial drug resistance, even in the current
treatments. Nowadays, several genes are related to the antimalarial resistance and effectiveness
in Plasmodium falciparum such as Plasmodium falciparum chloroquine transporter (pfcrt),
Plasmodium falciparum dihydrofolate reductase (pfdhfr), Plasmodium falciparum dihydropteroate
synthase (pfdhps), Plasmodium falciparum Kelch 13 gene (pfk13) and Plasmodium falciparum
multidrug resistance 1 (pfmdr1). Mutations at the pfcrt are related to chloroquine resistance and
are known to modulate other antimalarial drugs response, being disseminated among all the
endemic malaria countries [113], [114]. Mutations at the pfdhfr and pfdhps confer resistance to
pyrimethamine and sulphadoxine, respectively, with high alleles mutated globally widespread
[115], [116] [117], [118]. Mutations at the pfk13 have been shown to confer artemisinin tolerance
and are emergent in the African region [119]. Mutations and gene copy numbers at the pfmdr1
gene are associated with the resistance of several antimalarials including chloroquine lumefantrine
and mefloquine [120]. According to the World Health Organization, this is a threat to the elimination
of malaria disease.
21

1.7.2. Present Antimalarial treatment do not target vector transmission
According to the Malaria Eradication Agenda, future therapies involve the combinination of drugs
that target the symptomatic stage, such as most of the available antimalarials, but also, the less
metabolically active and non-replicating gametocytes that block human mosquito transmission
[121]. Recent studies show that only a small portion of antimalarials can target gametocytes. As
advised by WHO, primaquine is the only clinical treatment available that kills maturegametocytes
(I - V) [122]. Several studies show that most antimalarials cause environmental stress, thus
increasing the gametocyte's commitment and stimulating the dissemination of the disease[123].
1.7.3. The Key to the Interruption of Vector Transmission
The key to addressing gametocytes in the human body is by targeting the molecular pathways
responsible for the transcriptional regulation of sexual commitment (Figure 19) [124]. Chloroquine,
a 4-aminoquinoline drug, disrupts hemoglobin is catabolism, leading to the accumulation of free
heme, which is highly toxic to the parasites [125], [126], [127]. However, chloroquine is ineffective
against gametocytes in stage V, as their development is slower [128]. Only gametocytes stages I
to IV fully consume host hemoglobin, making them immature and susceptible to chloroquine [129],
[130]. Other 4-aminoquinolines like piperaquine and amodiaquine demonstrate efficacy against
gametocytes in vitro [92]. Piperaquine targets gametocytes stages II and III in vitro cultures, while
amodiaquine selectively kills immature gametocytes in vitro, inhibiting male ex-flagellation [92],
[131].

Interestingly, among a comprehensive list of drugs (Figure 20), only two are known to effectively
eliminate or prevent gametocyte formation and disease transmission: primaquine and methylene
blue [132]. Since the 1950s, primaquine has been demonstrated to directly kill mature
gametocytes, thereby preventing transmission to vectors. However, this treatment poses significant
toxicity risks for individuals with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, a frequent
genetic condition in malaria-endemic regions [133]. Tafenoquine represents a potentially safer
alternative to primaquine [134], [135]. Nonetheless, tafenoquine, which belongs to the same drug
family as primaquine, is also related to the risk of causing hemolytic toxicity in G6PD-deficient
individuals and others [134], [135]. In the 19th century, in Germany, malaria patients were
administered a synthetic dye known as methylene blue. While methylene blue exhibits potent
activity against all stages and genders of gametocytes (I – V) in vitro, its use is associated with side
effects and is not suitable for treating individuals with G6PD deficiency [136].
Therefore, the discovery of new drugs capable of effectively eliminating gametocytes and
interrupting vector transmission is essential for malaria control and elimination efforts.

AIMS

A foremost limitation related to the lack of drugs targeting the non-symptomatic and “neglected”
parasite gametocyte stage, is the challenge to produce large quantities and accurate quantification,
immensely reducing the capacity for high-throughput drug screening. Gametocytes are the key
within the parasite’s life cycle and in the dissemination of the disease. This master thesis aims to
generate a new transgenic parasite line for multi-stage drug screening, targeting the symptomatic
intra-erythrocyte parasite stage and most importantly, the gametocyte stage. Different genome-
editing strategies will be employed to modify the parasite transcriptional regulation of the sexual
commitment to induce gametocytogenesis, leading to large-scale gametocyte production. We also
aims to constitutively express fluorescent and/or luminescent protein in those lines, to generate a
P. falciparum reporter parasite for easy, fast, and accurate stage-specific quantification through
optical readout.
Specifically:
1. Transgene integration in the Plasmodium falciparum genome:
a) Engineer a transgenic parasite line that can increase sexual commitment
(gametocytogenesis). For this, the glmS ribozyme system will be used to generate a
conditional knockdown of the heterochromatic protein 1 (HP1), a natural repressor of the
PfAP2 – G transcription factor fundamental to sexual commitment. The SLI system will be
used to integrate the glmS cassette downstream of the pfhp1 gene.
b) Construct a Plasmodium falciparum reporter line for asexual and sexual parasite stages.
To detect the asexual forms, a green fluorescent protein (GFP) will be linked downstream
to the hp1 gene which will be silenced if the parasite is sexually committed. To detect
gametocytes, a red fluorescent protein-luciferase fusion (EmiRFP670Luc) will be expressed
under a gametocyte-stage specific promoter (pfs16), which will be integrated into the cg6
gene locus of the NF54attB parasite line through the attP-attB recombinase system. To be
able to use the attP-attB recombinase system, the CRISPR-Cas9 system will be used to
introduce the attB sequence in the cg6 locus of the NF54 parasite line.

4.2. Construction of a Plasmodium falciparum reporter line for the
asexual and sexual parasite stages
In addressing the urgent need for efficient gametocyte production and evaluation methods for
antimalarial drug research, it is crucial to overcome the challenges associated with accurately
identifying gametocytes amidst a mix of asexual stages. Presently, conventional methods are either
too laborious and subject to individual interpretation or fail to differentiate between asexual and
sexual forms effectively. To surmount this obstacle, the development of reporter lines may
represents promising solution. These lines incorporate genetic markers or reporter genes that
provide an easily identifiable readout, simplifying the process of gametocyte and asexual forms
identification. By introducing fluorescent proteins or luciferase genes specifically expressed in
gametocytes, researchers can achieve rapid and reliable detection, thus distinguishing sexual
forms from asexual stages with accuracy. Such reporter lines not only streamline the gametocyte
production process but also enhance the evaluation of antimalarial drug efficacy. With clear and
distinct markers indicating the presence of gametocytes, researchers can accurately assess the
impact of antimalarial treatments on transmission-stage parasites, facilitating the development of
more effective strategies to prevent disease transmission.
4.2.1 Engineering GFP cassette for P. falciparum intraerythrocytic asexual stage detection
The system described in section 4.1 targets the endogenous HP1 gene, which naturally
suppresses sexual conversion in parasites if activated. This gene is genetically tagged with a GFP,
enabling the visualization of asexual parasites through green fluorescence when HP1 is activated.
specifically, this system operates under the inducible glmS system. When HP1 is repressed, the
linked GFP cassette is also suppressed. Consequently, the knockdown of HP1 leads to the
activation of sexual commitment pathways within the parasites. This genetic tool allows for precise
control over sexual conversion processes, with the GPF solely being expressed in the asexual forms.

DISCUSSION

Malaria is one of the deadliest diseases in the world and is epidemic in 85 countries [2]. While
chemotherapy remains at the front-line for malaria treatment and prevention, present treatments
fail to tackle the disease transmission form – which specifically refers to the gametocytes stage of
the parasite’s life cycle. Gametocytes circulate in patients’ blood without causing clinical symptoms
and stand as a reservoir for transmission[137]. Thus, to control malaria, the discovery and
development of transmission-blocking drugs represent a medical need. Nevertheless, this requires
a deeper understanding of the gametocyte’s biology, thus depending on experimental methods for
large-scale gametocyte production, detection, and quantification. Currently, the standard method
for gametocyte production in vitro relies on the exposure of parasites to stress conditions including
high parasitaemia and nutrient starvation [74] .Furthermore, gametocytaemia is analyzed by labor-
intensive and user-dependent light microscopy. Therefore, herein, we developed a genetic-based
tool for gametocyte identification and quantification through fluorescence and/or luminescence.
This genetic tool comprises the pfs16 gametocytes-promoter (for expression of this biosensor in
gametocytes), EmiRFP670, and Luciferase reporter protein, and was obtained by using the
CRISPR-cas9 and Bxb1 integrase systems. This reporter line itself represents a novel tool for high-
throughput multistage drug screening, distinguishing between asexual parasites (marked by GFP)
and gametocytes (marked by EmiRFP670, and Luciferase) using different fluorescent proteins,
along with a conditional increase in sexual conversion facilitated by the gmlS ribozyme system.
Despite the construction of reporter lines incorporating fluorescent proteins into sexual proteins
throughout the literature, such as the GDV1 conditional expression tagged with GFP through FKBP
destabilization domains and activated with Shield-1 supplementation, still remains costly heavy and
with low specificity. Other studies have attempted to engineer reporter lines with cassettes to
identify proteins of interest or relevant stages but have faced limitations in accurately marking
asexual parasites and fully tracking gametocyte development [46]. There are some studies that
explore the engineering of reporter lines with a cassette attempting at identifying with fluorescence
the proteins of interest or the relevant stages. Researchers were able to create a fluorescent and
luminescent cassette (mCherry-luc@etramp10.3) that failed to mark asexual parasites and to follow
all the gametocytes development [138]. A study for quantitative assessment of the gametocyte’s
stages I, II, and III, failed to follow through the whole sexual development[139]. Although it is
important to be able to follow a signal between the five stages, stage V is the most relatable to
68

vector transmission, and therefore it is important to deepen our knowledge about it and focus on
tackling even more this gametocyte stage.
In this thesis, despite numerous endeavours aimed at establishing a resilient and economically
viable reporter line, our developed system, initiated with the HP1-GFP-glmS construct, proved to
not only facilitate the monitoring of asexual parasites through distinctive green fluorescence but
also grants the ability for the selective suppression of the upstream sexual repressor, HP1, under
specific conditions. Knocking down HP1 is expected to decrease 4 - to 5 - fold the HP1 expression,
and therefore increase sexual conversion [140]. Additionally, to track sexual conversion and
gametocytes, we aimed to insert a fluorescent and bioluminescent cassette into the cg6 (a non-
essential gene in the RBC stages) and utilize the sexual-specific promoter pfs16, known for its
reliability in detecting and quantifying gametocytes. While the acquisition of this transgenic line was
unsuccessful under the timeframe of this thesis, its potential remains promising. It holds the
prospect of enabling a more cost-effective, stage-specific, and dependable high-throughput
multistage drug screening approach. This reporter line stands as a pioneering tool for high-
throughput multistage drug screening, distinguishing asexual parasites (marked by GFP) and
gametocytes (indicated by EmiRFP670 and Luciferase) through distinct fluorescent and
luminescent proteins. Additionally, it incorporates a conditional enhancement of sexual conversion
facilitated by the glmS ribozyme system. While other constructed reporter lines add fluorescent
proteins to sexual proteins, such as the GDV1 conditional expression tagged with GFP via FKBP
destabilization domains, their maintenance with Shield-1 supplementation proves excessively costly
for laboratory upkeep [120][90]. In a recent 2020 study, researchers explored the potential of
inducing massive sexual conversion in parasites through the utilization of the Cre – LoxP system.
This approach allows for the conditional expression increase of pfap2-g, a crucial gene involved in
sexual conversion, by employing a specific strain containing the DiCre region. Recombination is
triggered upon induction by rapamycin through dimerization, leading to the desired effect [89]. In
a related study [88], glmS was employed as a complementary subexpression of GDV1 to promote
sexual conversion [121]. By conducting numerous attempts to develop a robust, feasible, and cost-
effective reporter line, our research aimed to create a reporter line that specifically targets asexual
parasites while addressing all stages of gametocyte development.
The bxb1 integrase system facilitates genome recombination by integrating an attB site into the
parasite genome, in this thesis specifically targeting the NF54 P. falciparum strain, known for its
propensity to produce gametocytes. To achieve this, we employed the CRISPR-Cas9 system, a
69

cutting-edge genetic-editing tool allowing precise genome insertion. However, this method posed
challenges, including difficulty in editing all parasites within a population and low transfection
efficiency in P. falciparum compared to other organisms[141]. While many researchers utilize a
two-plasmid system for CRISPR-Cas9, we optimized the process by employing a single-plasmid
approach, merging the Cas9 endonuclease cassette with the region of interest. Troubleshooting
involved adjusting vector concentration, employing technical adaptations, and optimizing
transformation conditions.
Post-transfection, genotyping proved a successful editing within some of the parasites, confirming
the presence of NF54 with attB sites to implement the golden attP – attB recombinase system.
This well-established method allows for rapid gene edits, including larger insertions compared to
CRISPR-Cas9, despite the requirement for an integrase. We propose the use fo this system to insert
a larger cassette containing a sexual-specific promoter expressing fluorescent and luciferase
reporter proteins. While the system has drawbacks, such as residual plasmid presence post-
insertion, it offers benefits like diverse gene modifications and low off-target recombination risks.
In this thesis, we introduce a workflow which features as backbone the plasmid pDC2-pfcam
p-
Snf7-GFP-BSD-attP, facilitating both genome integration and episomal expression. Genome
integration ensures stable and heritable expression across generations, eliminating the need for
continuous drug selection, unlike episomal expression, which requires constant maintenance. This
underscores the significance of genome integration in achieving reliable and enduring gene
expression in host organisms.
The pfs16 gene has emerged as a leading candidate for targeting gametocytes via reporter
products. Research indicates that pfs16 undergoes upregulation approximately 35 hours post-
invasion and serves as a specific sexual gene present throughout all five stages of gametocyte
development, maintaining consistent expression levels despite the presence of other genes[56].
By introducing the EmiRFP670Luc@s16 cassette into the parasites, we can effectively quantify and
detect gametocytes across their developmental stages, from early stages that resemble asexual
parasites under the microscope up to late stages. This transgenic line will enable detection through
both fluorescence and luminescence, providing a comprehensive tool for studying and monitoring
gametocyte dynamics.
In the dissertation timeline, the final objective of this thesis, which aimed to validate the newest
transgenic line, unfortunately, could not be achieved. As outlined, the HP1 – GFP – glmS edited
70

parasites were intended to undergo transfection with pL6 – cas 9 – yDHODH – 5´cg6 – attB –
3´cg6, to insert an attB site into the cg6 gene. Subsequently, a cassette containing
EmiRFP670Luc@s16 would have been introduced to track gametocytes.
Despite this setback, the new reporter line not only distinguishes asexual parasites through green
fluorescence but also enables conditional enhancement of sexual conversion. This allows for the
identification and quantification of gametocytes, marked by red fluorescence, at various stages of
RBC development. This tool addresses the dual challenge of enhancing sexual conversion in
Plasmodium falciparum parasites and accurately identifying and quantifying sexual versus asexual
parasites.
In addition to its fluorescent properties - by using this tool - gametocytes will express a
bioluminescent reporter protein, which complements the limitations of fluorescence by reducing
signal noise and enhancing its sensitivity. Fluorescence will be particularly valuable for assessing
drug effects, by visualizing red fluorescent gametocytes, and identifying the localization of pfs16
within them through live imaging.
Comparing fluorescence to luminescence, luminescence involves the emission of light by a
substance without the need for an external light source, while fluorescence specifically refers to
the absorption and re-emission of light at a longer wavelength. Luminescence assays tend to be
highly sensitive and stable, whereas fluorescence offers high brightness and spatial resolution,
making it ideal for imaging applications[107].

homology-directed recombination. Additionally, the successful creation of the pDC2 – pfs16 –
EmiRFP670 – Luc – bsd – attP plasmid was accomplished. Despite the successful integration of
the attB site, a contamination led to the loss of the mutant bulk culture, hindering the completion
of the NF54attB construction. Furthermore, optimization of a stress protocol for gametocyte
production was completed, in order to ensure quantitative and qualitative results for gametocytes
production. However, the need to induce sexual commitment to trigger the appearance of
gametocytes and optimize gametocyte detection through optical readout requires further
investigations.
Moving forward, the future perspective of this work is to generate the HP1 – glmS – GFP attB
transgenic line to produce gametocytes in high quantities through conditional knockdown of HP1,
an upstream repressor of sexual differentiation. Integration into the genome using the attP – attB
recombinase system, along with the transgene cassette EmiRFP670Luc@s16, will enable the
detection and quantification of gametocytes for high-throughput multistage drug screening via
optical readouts. The Plasmodium falciparum NF54 HP1 – glmS – GFP EmiRFP670Luc@s16
transgenic line remains a promise for the development of new therapeutics targeting both asexual
parasites and gametocytes, thereby preventing malaria transmission.
This research is in line with the goals set forth by the World Health Organization (WHO) to combat
the disease. By possessing a transgenic parasite line capable of screening every drug effect in
gametocytes and asexual RBC stages, research and innovation in malaria treatment will be
enriched, potentially leading to the discovery of revolutionary treatments for malaria.
