Degeneração Noradrenérgica num Modelo de Murganho DJ-1
Knockout
Resumo
A doença de Parkinson (DP) é a segunda doença neurodegenerativa mais comum, com
aproximadamente 10% de casos familiares, causada por várias mutações identificadas, como no gene
DJ-1. Os modelos de murganho com ablação para o gene DJ-1 (KO) demonstraram apresentar défices
progressivos de comportamento motor, sem alterações significativas no sistema dopaminérgico
nigroestriatal, descrito como o substrato neuropatológico da DP. A degeneração de um núcleo
noradrenérgico - Locus Ceruleus (LC) - tem sido associada ao desenvolvimento de sintomas não motores
e ao agravamento dos sintomas motores. Neste trabalho, o nosso objetivo é estudar o envolvimento da
degeneração do LC num modelo DJ-1 KO, analisando tanto o fenótipo motor quanto o não motor. Para
isso, administrámos uma neurotoxina noradrenérgica seletiva (DSP-4) em murganhos DJ-1 KO. Primeiro,
avaliámos a dose apropriada. Para avaliar as alterações funcionais, realizámos uma bateria de testes
que abrangem vários sintomas associados à DP, incluindo depressão, ansiedade, défice cognitivo,
alteração da atividade locomotora e disfunção da marcha. Para avaliar os efeitos histológicos realizámos
imuno-histoquímica para a Tirosina Hidroxilase (TH) no LC, substancia nigra (SNpc) e estriado (STR).
Os animais com ablação da DJ-1 mostraram uma tendência para alterações na função motora,
disfunção da marcha e para ansiedade relacionada com novidade. Os animais DJ-1 tratados com DSP-4
apresentaram capacidade de natação prejudicada, disfunção na marcha, tendência para um
comportamento semelhante à depressão, e um melhor desempenho na reaquisição da memória. Estes
animais mostraram uma redução de 42.6% na densitometria no estriado e uma diminuição no número
de células TH+ na SNpc em comparação com murganhos Wild-type (WT). A injeção de DSP-4 levou à
diminuição de 83-85% dos neurónios no LC. A administração de DSP-4 em murganhos DJ-1 resultou
numa diminuição de 58% das fibras dopaminérgicas no estriado, sem afetar os neurónios nigrais.
Neste trabalho, desenvolvemos um modelo capaz de recapitular a degeneração noradrenérgica
descrita na DP, que levou a degeneração nigroestriatal significativa, com um fenótipo comportamental
ligeiro, a nível motor e não motor. Este modelo pode ser uma abordagem adequada para o estudo de
terapias neuroprotetoras focadas nos neurónios noradrenérgicos do LC.
Palavras-Chave
Locus Ceruleus, DJ-1 Knockout, Doença de Parkinson, DSP-4, noradrenalina.

Noradrenergic Degeneration in a DJ-1 Knockout Mouse Model
Abstract
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, with
approximately 10% familial cases, caused by several identified mutations, such as in the DJ-1 gene. DJ-
1 knockout (KO) mouse models have been shown to present progressive motor behavioral deficits,
without significant alterations in the nigrostriatal dopaminergic system, described as the
neuropathological substrate of PD. Degeneration of the noradrenergic nucleus - locus coeruleus (LC) -
has been linked to the development of non-motor symptoms and to the aggravation of motor symptoms.
Herein, we aimed to study the involvement of LC degeneration in a DJ-1 KO model looking at both motor
and non-motor phenotype. For that, we have administered a selective noradrenergic neurotoxin (DSP-4)
in DJ-1 KO mice. First, we optimized the appropriate dosage to be administered. To assess functional
alterations, we have performed a battery of behavioral tests covering several symptoms associated with
PD, including depression, anxiety, cognitive impairments, impaired locomotor activity and gait
dysfunction. To assess the histological effects of both DSP-4 and the mutation we have performed
Tyrosine Hydroxylase (TH) immunohistochemistry on the LC, substantia nigra (SNpc), and striatum (STR).
DJ-1 animals have shown tendencies to impaired motor functioning, gait dysfunction and a
tendency to develop anxiety-related to novelty. DJ-1 KO treated with DSP-4 showed impaired swimming
ability, gait dysfunction, tendency for depressive-like behavior, and improved fear memory retrieval. DJ-1
animals showed a reduction of 42.6% in striatal densitometry and a decrease in the number of TH+ cells
in the SNpc compared to WT littermates. The injection of DSP-4 led to a decrease of 83-85% neurons
within the LC. DSP-4 administration in DJ-1 mice led to a 58% decrease in dopaminergic fibers content
in the striatum but did not affect nigral neurons.
In this work, we developed a model capable of recapitulating noradrenergic degeneration described
in PD, which led to significant nigrostriatal degeneration, and was translated into mild behavioral
phenotype, at the motor and non-motor level. This model may provide a suitable approach for the study
of neuroprotective therapies focused on the LC noradrenergic neurons.
KEYWORDS
Locus Coeruleus, DJ-1 Knockout, DSP-4, Parkinson’s disease, noradrenaline.

1. INTRODUCTION
Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is the most common movement disorder and the second most common
neurodegenerative disorder affecting more than 10 million people worldwide1,2. PD is strongly correlated
with age2,3. It is rare before 60 years old (≈1%) but at the age of 80 the prevalence rises to about 3%2.
Pathologically, it is characterized by degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars
compacta (SNpc) and dopamine depletion in the striatum (STR), which leads to changes in the functioning
of downstream basal ganglia circuitry1,4. Motor impairment, i.e. parkinsonism is the main feature that
clinically characterizes PD, being characterized by bradykinesia in combination with either rest tremor,
rigidity, or both4,5. The dopamine precursor amino acid L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA) was first
established in 1950 as an attempt to replace striatal dopamine depletion and remains as the "gold
standard" symptomatic therapy for PD1,4. Still, disease-modifying treatments that halt the gradual
degeneration of dopaminergic neurons remain an unmet need.
Figure 1. Progression of PD with age3,6,7. Several studies report that PD may develop from the olfactory or
gastrointestinal tract, and several genetic mutations and environmental factors have been identified as a risk factor for the
development of PD8–11. Preclinical stage comprises the presence of neurodegeneration before the manifestation of clinical
symptoms, which sets the beginning of prodromal stage3,4,7,12. This stage is marked by a broad range of symptoms that may
include anxiety, depression, sleep problems, cognitive impairments, and constipation6,13. Finally, in the clinical stage, the
diagnosis of PD is made upon the establishment of cardinal motor symptoms, such as bradykinesia and resting tremors3.

1

Recently, an increasing body of knowledge has shown that before the appearance of the motor
signs that allow PD diagnosis, patients experience non-motor symptoms and subtle motor alterations
(figure 1)3–5. This prodromal stage of PD has been correlated with degeneration in other neurotransmitter
systems besides the dopaminergic nigrostriatal pathway1,4,5,14. Identifying specific clinical markers
associated with prodromal PD is crucial for early detection and intervention5,7,15. Motor symptoms
associated with prodromal PD may comprise subtle changes in movement coordination, mild tremors, or
alterations in posture4,5,7. On the other hand, non-motor symptoms are more evident and diverse and can
include cognitive disfunctions (such as dementia), sleep disorders, depression, and autonomic
dysfunction1,5,6,16.
Therefore, to better understand PD progression it is critical to understand how different non-
dopaminergic brain areas are affected in PD. A structure that has been proposed to be early affected in
PD is the Locus Coeruleus (LC)13,17. This brainstem nucleus is involved in various functions, including
regulation of arousal, attention, and stress responses8,18. LC degeneration is correlated with the
development and aggravation of both motor and non-motor PD symptoms8,13,15. Despite its potential
implication in the early stages of PD, the LC remains mostly understudied. Further research is needed to
explore its involvement in prodromal PD and understand the underlying mechanisms contributing to its
degeneration. Moreover, studying the LC could provide new insights into early diagnostic techniques and
novel therapeutic strategies.
Additionally to understanding the degenerative processes and regions affected during the course
of PD, it is important to acquire insight into how genetic mutations may influence vulnerability to
degeneration9,10. Genetic factors have been increasingly recognized as part of PD etiology4,9,10. Investigating
the genetic basis of PD may provide insights into disease mechanisms, risk assessment, early diagnosis,
and the development of targeted therapies9,10,19. Even though most cases of PD are idiopathic, the discovery
of rare PD forms with Mendelian inheritance (5-10% of PD cases) sparked interest in the role of genetics
in PD. Recent studies have also revealed the existence of polymorphic genes that can contribute to the
development of sporadic PD or increase its risk and progression10,11,19–21. Some mutations associated with
PD include autosomal-dominant (LRRK2 and SNCA) and autosomal-recessive forms (Parkin, PINK1 and
DJ-1) and risk mutations (GBA) 9–11. A list of genes and associated mutations is presented in table 1.

Autosomal recessive mutations in the PARK7 gene, which encodes for the DJ-1 protein, accounts
for about 1-2% of early-onset PD cases2,9–11. DJ-1 is a highly conserved protein (189 amino acids) which
regulates numerous processes important in neuroprotection, such as anti-oxidative, anti-apoptotic and
anti-inflammatory pathways9,10,22. Patients with DJ-1 mutations exhibit an early onset of rigidity, tremors,
dyskinesia and generally have a good response to L-DOPA treatment11. Moreover, DJ-1-linked PD cases
often present non-motor symptomatology, like anxiety and cognitive deficits and have shown the presence
3

of degeneration in the LC9,23. However, there is still a lack of description regarding the alterations, the
extent of the degeneration and its correlation with the development and exacerbation of symptoms.
Studying DJ-1 mutation can provide valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of PD pathology,
particularly in individuals with an early onset of the disease.
In this section, we will provide a description about the LC, its functioning, and studies performed
regarding its involvement in PD symptomatology. We will address DJ-1 mutation and mechanisms
potentially involved in PD pathogenesis, as well as review the studies using DJ-1 knockout (KO) animals.
1.1 Locus Coeruleus
The LC is a structure located in the caudal central gray pons under the floor of the fourth ventricle
with widespread projections to several brain regions such as olfactory bulb, hippocampus, hypothalamus,
striatum, cerebral cortex, cerebellum, subthalamic nucleus and spinal cord (figure 2)8,18. Due to its
location deep in the brainstem and its small size, it has been difficult to assess LC neuronal activity and
understand its correlation with several aspects of the disease8,13,18. Advances in magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI), optogenetics, chemogenetics, RNA sequencing and specific labeling techniques have
improved the knowledge regarding LC functioning and anatomy8,12. Immunolabeling techniques using
tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and dopamine-ꞵ-hydroxylase (DBH), critical enzymes of noradrenaline
biosynthesis, show that LC is mainly composed of noradrenergic neurons, being recognized as the major
source of noradrenaline in the brain8,18.
Noradrenaline (NA) is a neurotransmitter involved in a wide range of behavioral and physiological
processes including wakefulness, arousal, attention, mood, memory, appetite and homeostasis8,24 During
several years the LC noradrenergic system has been described as an homogeneous system with unified
action. Recent evidence supports that this is an heterogeneous system with different morphologies and
actions, depending on firing mode17,25. LC neurons are mostly medium sized with several long and thin
dendrites of the central part of the LC. Smaller spindle-shaped neurons are present in the sub coeruleus
region, mainly targeting the brainstem and spinal cord26. NA neurons can fire in two different modes, tonic
and phasic, and the capacity of switching between these regulate different behavioral states18. Tonic firing
is related to the arousal and waking state whereas phasic mode is activated during focused task
performance and highly precise behavioral responses18.
NA has several effects on target neurons, including modulation of intracellular cascades, cellular
excitability, membrane potentials and synaptic plasticity, which in turn lead to the modulation of pain,
4

motor control, energy homeostasis and control of local blood flow26. Noradrenergic signaling happens over
three major categories of receptors, α1, α2 and ꞵ, with different subtypes that elicit different cellular
responses24. NA also exhibits potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties in the brain27. NA can
exert neuroprotective effects by decreasing the release of brain related trophic factors and reducing the
generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)12,17,28. Lesioning of the LC and consequent NA depletion has
been shown to affect microglial function and lead to an increase of oxidative stress12,13,18. Importantly, NA
depletion in the LC seems to exacerbate dopaminergic neurons loss in the SNpc, contributing to the
progression of the disease. This has been shown in several animal models combining LC lesions with
oxidative stress insults, such as 6-Hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA), 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-
tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) and genetic models, such as DJ-1 KO7,24. Moreover, NA has been shown to
exert paracrine effects on neurons, glial cells and microvessels, thus confirming a large involvement of
this system not only by typical synapses but by extra synaptic interactions18,26.

1.1.1 Locus Coeruleus and Parkinson´s Disease: a focus on non-motor symptoms
5

Neuronal loss within LC has been described in several studies17. With age, the number of LC cells
and NA concentration declines. By the age of 90 years, 25% of LC neurons are lost and NA levels in the
brain are reduced by 50%31. Clinical studies in PD patients revealed that degeneration of LC noradrenergic
neurons occurs earlier and to a greater extent than the loss of nigral dopaminergic neurons within the
SNpc7,13,15,32. Some studies reported an 83% loss of noradrenergic innervation in various targets of the LC-
NA system compared to a 78% DA degeneration in the onset of PD motor symptoms14. The degeneration
of LC NA neurons seems to be directly linked to early non-motor symptoms17,31. Non-motor symptoms
related to LC degeneration that occur before the onset of motor symptoms may be important clinical
markers of this prodromal phase of the disease15,31,32. Therefore, it is important to address these non-motor
symptoms and how LC dysfunction might contribute to their occurrence.
Depression and anxiety
Mood disorders, such as depression and anxiety, are frequent symptoms experienced among PD
patients (25-40%) and have been recognized as a clinical marker of developing PD24,33,34. People who
develop depression early in the course of PD are more likely to have higher deterioration of motor function
and disability over time6,34. Antidepressant drugs have been demonstrated to be useful in the treatment of
parkinsonian depression. Dopaminergic neurons within the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and nucleus
accumbens (NAc) are described to have fundamental roles in motivation and reward, which are usually
impaired in several psychiatric disorders such as depression35–37. It has been suggested that noradrenergic
neurons of the LC and dopaminergic neurons of the VTA work together on regulating behavior since they
both converge in the prefrontal cortex38. Some studies showed a correlation between the decline of NA
content in VTA, the reduction of LC pigmentation, and the reduction of NA transporter binding with the
prevalence of depression in PD patients14,24,35. Drugs that increase NA and serotonin (5-HT) levels act as
antidepressants, whereas the depletion of NA or 5-HT may induce a depressive state14.
Like depression, anxiety can arise at any time over the course of PD6,34. In PD, anxiety most
commonly manifests as generalized anxiety disorder or panic disorder6,34. The LC has been studied in the
context of anxiety-related disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)39,40. Tonic and
continuous activity of the LC is high in states of distress or anxiety41. Studies in rodents demonstrate that
the LC is involved in several types of stress response, leading to increased anxiety and defensive
behaviors41. Increased LC activity, along with an augmented TH expression, has been associated with the
onset of anxiety-like behavior in rodents, particularly related to exposure to stressful events (i.e. restraint)
or in response to fear34,41. However, there is contradictory evidence suggesting that a reduced LC activity
6

is associated with anxiety41–43. When lesioning the LC, some studies have reported an increase of anxiety
behavior related to a reduced exploration of novel environments6. While the LC has a global function in
mediating arousal to strongly aversive stimuli, distinct modulations can have different and opposite
functional roles in mediating learning or responses to aversive stimuli6,15,33. Therefore, the distinct roles of
the LC-NA system and the specific evoked behavioral responses require further study.
Cognitive disfunctions
Although cognitive impairments become more regular and prominent with the progression of the
disease, almost 25% of the patients have developed mild cognitive impairments in early stages of PD6,18.
Patients in early-stage PD have shown an impairment in tasks requiring behavioral shift, which is highly
dependent of NA modulation8,18. A study conducted in PD patients with mild cognitive impairment showed
a lower LC MRI signal and a decreased performance on cognitive flexibility tests26,44. Consistent with this,
treatment with NA re-uptake inhibitors like atomoxetine showed an improvement in cognitive performance
among PD patients44. Recent research has pointed towards the involvement of LC noradrenergic system
in learning and memory formation, sensory processing, synaptic plasticity, network resetting and decision
making6,15,26,32.
Learning and memory formation involves multiple neural systems27,44. These include hippocampal
context-dependent associative learning, stimulus discrimination, declarative and working memory, and
amygdala-mediated affective learning6,27,44. The LC has been proposed to exert effect in these neuronal
systems6,45,46. The LC-NA projections to the hippocampus regulate long-term potentiation and hippocampal
plasticity, positively influencing the process of memory consolidation and retrieval6,45. The LC also
modulates the interaction between the hippocampus and the amygdala, related to emotional memory
and stress response, mainly in passive-avoidance memory consolidation6.
Phasic activation of NA LC neurons is critical in focus and attention18,26. The administration of
clonidine, an α2-receptor agonist, inhibits the release of NA and leads to impaired performance in high
arousal and high demand tasks 24. In non-human primates optimal levels of NA in the prefrontal cortex
are key for high arousal and working-memory tasks12,47. The effects of clonidine on humans, acquired by
positron emission tomography (PET), have shown the interference of the LC in the network that mediates
attention and working memory26,47. Taken together these findings support the assumption that the LC may
have powerful effect on the regulation of multiple memory systems, including hippocampal plasticity,
working memory, and amygdala-mediated associative learning for aversive events6,44.
7

Sleep Disturbances
Another common symptom among PD patients is sleep disturbances, mainly rapid eye movement
(REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD) which has been also proposed to be one of the main clinical markers
for prodromal PD7,18,32. The LC plays a main role in the sleep-waking cycle, anticipating activity fluctuation
and promoting a state of vigilance. The LC-NA system regulates the circadian rhythm of expression of
genes responsible for the waking state13,29. During REM sleep, when the LC activity is suppressed, there
is a reduction in the expression of these genes13. In agreement with this, lesioning the LC ablates the
waking-related genes expression13. Studies performed in animals and humans have also shown that LC
is one of the effectors of the orexin/hypocretin neurons of the lateral hypothalamus which are involved in
arousal and inhibition of REM sleep48.
Clinical, imaging and neuropathological evidence demonstrate that LC-NA system is involved early
in the sequence of the pathological alterations leading to sleep disturbances several years before
dopaminergic degeneration is noticed29. In healthy patients, during quiet wakefulness, LC neurons fire at
a regular slow rate, whereas during REM these neurons are completely silent8,26. PD patients with RBD
showed a decrease in signal intensity of the LC when compared with PD patients without RBD48. The
authors suggested that only a certain degeneration in the LC noradrenergic system could lead to RBD,
however, this correlation is not properly established, and further studies are required.
Pain
Pain is one of the most common symptoms displayed by PD patients (40%) and is often
considered worse than motor symptoms30. The LC noradrenergic system plays a critical role in central
modulation of pain due its descending projections to the spinal cord49,50. Descending projections from the
LC interact with pain responsive neurons of trigeminal sensory nuclei and the spinal dorsal horn, altering
neuronal sensitivity and modulating nociceptive transmission and the experience of pain50. The
antinociceptive effects of norepinephrine are primarily mediated through α2-adrenergic receptors on the
central terminals and the subsequent activation of secondary projection neurons. Descending
noradrenergic projections from the LC can inhibit the sensation of pain and pharmacological inhibition of
spinal noradrenergic transmission results in decreased nociceptive thresholds. It is hypothesized that
dysfunctional descending noradrenergic pathways might contribute to central neuropathic pain (CNP)49,50.
Autonomic dysfunction
The autonomic nervous system (ANS) serves as a communication route between the central
8

nervous system (CNS) and peripheral tissues51. Several autonomic dysfunctionalities have been described
to appear in early-stage PD patients, which include gastrointestinal malfunction, cardiovascular
dysregulation, urinary disturbance and sexual dysfunction6,52. It should be mentioned that these symptoms
can occur independently, and their severity can vary between patients51. One example of cardiovascular
dysfunction is orthostatic hypotension (OH) which has been estimated to have a prevalence of
approximately 30% in early-stage PD patients52. The LC has strong connections with sympathetic
preganglionic neurons of the spinal cord, which control autonomic functions16. PET imaging showed that
there is a significant loss of NA uptake in cardiac postganglionic sympathetic neurons in early PD which
could contribute to cardiovascular dysregulation47,52. In line with this, pathological examination of
sympathetic nerves supplying the heart in patients with PD revealed a substantial loss of noradrenergic
fibers51. LC degeneration might contribute and influence these dysfunctionalities but there is still no clear
evidence of this involvement.
In summary, the LC seems to be involved in a variety of non-motor symptoms often described in
PD patients and proposed to be related to a prodromal stage. Understanding the role of the LC in both
PD patients and healthy people is important to identify possible clinical markers and allow early PD
diagnosis.
1.1.2 Locus Coeruleus and Parkinson’s Disease: experimental models (DSP-4)
The current experimental models of PD are mainly focused on reproducing the nigrostriatal DA
degeneration, not providing much information about the involvement of other neurotransmitter systems1,53.
In the last decade, some studies have started to explore the involvement of LC-NA system in the
pathophysiology of PD16,32,54. Targeting the noradrenergic system in neurodegenerative diseases such as
AD and PD has received greater attention and several models of noradrenergic degeneration have been
generated. N-(2-chloroethyl)-N-ethyl-2-bromobenzylamine (DSP-4) is a highly selective neurotoxin for
noradrenergic projections originating from the LC and has been widely used to study the LC-NA system50,55–
59.
Mode of Action
Figure 3 summarizes the current understanding of DSP-4 mode of action. DSP-4 crosses the
blood-brain barrier and is cyclized into a reactive aziridinium ion55,57,60. The aziridinium ion is transported
by the NA transporter into the NAergic nerve terminals where it is accumulated, triggering
degeneration55,60. DSP-4 also inhibits its own uptake by an irreversible mechanism at an inner site of the
NAT57,60,60. The difference in the capacity of transport and inhibition of the transport becomes a critical and
9

limiting factor determining the amount of the toxin entering the neurons60. Inside these neurons the
aziridinium ion reacts with cellular components, producing a toxic reaction14,60. The first sign of this toxicity
is a rapid decline of NA levels in the nerve terminals and axons60. Based on experiments using DSP-4, two
separate subsystems of noradrenergic fibers were described, the LC and non-coerulean noradrenergic
fibers, which differ not only in their projections but also with respect to the pharmacological properties of
their axon terminals61. DSP-4 is proposed to affect specifically LC noradrenergic fibers by inducing
morphological changes, inhibiting norepinephrine reuptake, stimulating NA release, and increasing
turnover rate of NA56. DSP-4 has been proposed to solely affect the noradrenergic fibers from the LC55,60,61.
It has been suggested that this selectivity toward LC neurons may be related to the significant difference
found in the affinity of DSP-4 for the NA uptake carrier in different brain structures61. On the molecular
level a cellular energy collapse by lack of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) may be an explanation of DSP-4
neurotoxicity55. Another hypothesis is that DSP-4 downregulates the noradrenergic phenotypes by its
actions on DNA replication, leading to replication stress and cell cycle arrest57,60. Markers of terminal loss
are observed as a reduction in NA brain tissue content, decrease in NAT density, and temporary increase
in α2-adrenergic auto receptor60,61. However, the mechanism underlying this effect is yet to be fully
understood.

Effects of DSP-4 Administration in Rodents
Several studies in rodents showed that systemic administration of DSP-4 has a dose-dependent
depleting effect on NA levels in several brain regions and spinal cord27,55,56,58,62. The cerebellum, brainstem,
mesencephalon, and pons-medulla seem to be the most affected brain areas, while in the thalamus and
medulla oblongata no significant alterations are noted60. In the central nervous system the depletion is
long lasting while in the peripheral sympathetic nervous system after a month there is an almost complete
recovery of NA innervation60,63. Different dosages of DSP-4 have been used in studies to induce the PD-like
phenotypes in mice, with typical dosages ranging from 6mg/kg to 50mg/kg55,61,63. Lower dosages (6mg/kg)
produce a partial lesion while higher dosages (50mg/kg) induce neurotoxicity and about 90% reduction
of NA content14,55,60,64. Still, it is not clear whether DSP-4 leads to a complete ablation of NA neurons57,61.
Some studies have pointed to a decrease in the level of NA but not to a loss of noradrenergic neurons50.
DSP-4 LC lesions also seems to indirectly affect the dopaminergic system58,64. In a study conducted in
rats, lesioning the LC with DSP-4 lead to a decrease in DA turnover, which supports the role of the LC-NA
system in neuroprotection64. A summary of the studies conducted using DSP-4 treatment is outlined in
Table 2.
In addition to investigating the integrity of the LC-NA system and its connection with other
monoaminergic systems and brain regions, certain studies have also examined the motor and non-motor
behavior in rodents with DSP-4-induced LC lesions.
When administered alone, DSP-4 does not lead to any evident altered motor functioning14.
However, when combined with other neurotoxins, such as MPTP and 6-OHDA, DSP-4 leads to an
exacerbation of neuronal degeneration and potentiates the appearance motor symptoms58,65–67. DSP-4
lesions on MPTP rats significantly aggravated the motor deficits and histological analysis on these animals
showed a significant reduction on TH-positive cells in the SNpc ipsilateral to the lesioned LC, indicating
that lesioning LC increases DA neurons degeneration susceptibility14,27. According with this, studies
performed in 6-OHDA animals treated with DSP-4 showed an aggravation on the forelimb akinesia and a
reduction in dopaminergic neurons in animals with loss of LC-NA neurons7,17,25. This suggests that the
degeneration of LC-NA neurons may act as a potentiator for DA degeneration, exacerbating the
progression of PD symptomatology58,64. However, in other studies no alterations on motor function were
11

observed and further studies assessing the effect of NA depletion in motor function are required64,66,68.
Regarding non-motor symptoms, DSP-4 lesioning have shown to impair learning and memory
tasks with a reduction of about 70% in LC-NA neurons27,45. Some studies reported an increase in anxious
and depressive-like behavior, neophobia, increased aggressiveness and lack of fear factors14,27,50,55,58,69.
Aligned with this, histologically, DSP-4 not only provoked a marked reduction in LC-NA neurons, but also
a significant reduction of NA levels in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, which may be an
explanation for the deficits observed in the behavioral analysis.

1.2 DJ-1
In humans, DJ-1 mutation generally leads to an early onset, slowly progressive, parkinsonism73. DJ-
1 is highly expressed in cells with high energy demands, like neurons and astrocytes, and therefore cells
that produce high levels of reactive oxygen species23,65.
DJ-1 is oxidatively damaged in sporadic PD patient’s brains, contributing to the hypothesis that DJ-
1 does play a role in neuroprotection against oxidative stress. In vitro, it has been shown that intrinsic
13

loss of DJ-1 in primary dopaminergic neurons is sufficient to confer hypersensitivity to oxidative stress23,74.
Patient-derived DJ-1 deficient cells cellular phenotypes are mainly mitochondrial dysfunction and a
reduced dopaminergic differentiation, supporting the notion that PD pathogenesis involves an important
metabolic component75. Given its growing clinical relevance, DJ-1 levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)
have been studied as a potential marker for neurodegenerative diseases such as PD and Amyotrophic
lateral sclerosis (ALS)23,74. CSF DJ-1 has shown to be increased in PD patients and seems to increase with
age, however, age dependence may become weaker during the course of PD. CSF DJ-1 levels are elevated
in early stages of the disease, being suggested to play a protective role against oxidative stress during PD
onset74.
To study the role of DJ-1 in PD researchers have generated animal models that lack functional DJ-
1 protein by knocking out the DJ-1 gene. These DJ-1 KO mouse models are useful for studying the effects
of DJ-1 deficiency on the development of PD-like symptoms.
1.2.1 Mechanisms
Although there has been clinical evidence suggesting the involvement of DJ-1 in the development
and aggravation of PD, there are contradictory explanations regarding the underlying mechanisms
responsible for these alterations. Several pathways have been implicated in its function, particularly in
the context of PD. Figure 4 represents relevant pathways studied in the context of PD in which DJ-1 is
being proposed to participate.
DJ-1 has been widely studied to be involved in protecting cells from oxidative stress, one of the
main mechanisms that has been correlated with the development of PD1,4. In healthy dopaminergic
neurons DJ-1 is mostly localized in the cytoplasm76. When exposed to elevated levels of ROS, the DJ-1
protein oxidises to its Cys-SOH form and is translocated to the mitochondria and the nucleus. In the
2
mitochondria, DJ-1 levels seem to increase in response to oxidative stress, in order to supress ROS
accumulation76,77. Thus, it has been hypothesized that DJ-1 may contribute to prevent oxidative damage
in dopaminergic neurons78. DJ-1 also localizes at the outer membrane of the mitochondria and contributes
to maintain a healthy mitochondrial environment74. Loss of DJ-1 has been shown to lead to impaired
autophagy and dysfunctional mitochondria74,75. Cytologic analyses of fibroblasts isolated from PD patients
with DJ-1 mutations showed impaired mitochondrial functioning and an increased sensitivity to ROS76.
Still, DJ -1 is not as efficient in removing ROS as other enzymes involved in oxidative homeostasis76. Given
this, several studies have explored DJ-1 as a potential oxidative stress sensor, which modulates signalling
pathways and the expression of genes involved in neuroprotective and neuroinflammatory pathways76,79.
14

For instance, studies performed in DA neurons exposed to 6-OHDA, and hydrogen peroxide (HO) have
2 2
shown that increased levels of DJ-1 induce Glutathione (GSH) synthesis, an antioxidant protein, improving
neuroprotection74,80.

DJ-1 is also known to regulate several transcription factors (TFs) that are relevant in PD23. DJ-1
can interfere with dopamine synthesis by interacting with the polypyrimidine tract binding protein and
associated splicing factor (PSF)23,76,80. DJ-1 stimulates the acetylation of TH-promoter-bound histones by
blocking the SUMOylating of the transcriptional co-repressor PSF82. DJ-1 activates dopamine synthesis by
up-regulating the transcription of TH, the enzyme responsible for converting L-tyrosine to L-DOPA74.
Inactivation of DJ-1 results in loss of TH expression and L-DOPA production, leading to impaired
15

nigrostriatal functioning and therefore contributing to the development of PD74. Moreover, DJ-1 directly
regulates TH activity, exerting a positive effect on TH expression. With age, DJ-1 oxidized levels increase
and its activity toward TH decreases, being one of the key factors for the onset of PD80,82. DJ-1 also
contributes to the reuptake of dopamine into synaptic vesicles by interacting directly with the vesicular
monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2)78,80. VMAT2 reuptakes excess dopamine into synaptic vesicles,
protecting neurons from oxidized dopamine-induced toxicity. DJ-1 deficiency has been shown to impair
dopamine reuptake, therefore contributing to oxidative stress80.
DJ-1 participates in maintaining cellular redox balance by influencing the levels of various
antioxidants and oxidants76,79. For instance, DJ-1 is thought to have a role in the extracellular signal-
regulated kinase ½ (ERK½) pathway, which has functions in antioxidant response and dopamine
homeostasis23,79,80. DJ-1 increases ERK½ phosphorylation, activating antioxidant mechanisms. Another
example is the DJ-1 interaction with the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) which interacts
with antioxidant response element that regulates the expression of certain antioxidant genes, a
mechanism of relevance in response to oxidative stress79. Some studies have shown that DJ-1 is able to
stabilize and facilitate the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, increasing the expression of antioxidant
enzymes23,80. Herein, it is though that DJ-1 mutations can lead to cellular dysfunction and neuronal damage
due to dysregulation of redox homeostasis79,80.
DJ-1 is implicated in modulating inflammatory responses in the brain. It can regulate the
production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibit the activation of inflammatory pathways, indirectly
contributing to neuronal survival23,81. DJ-1 loss increases intracellular ROS, nitric oxide (NO), interleukin 6
(IL-6), and interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), reduces the anti-inflammatory factor triggering receptor expressed
on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), blights migration capacity, and enhances phagocytosis23. DJ-1 KO attenuates
astrocytic anti-inflammatory activity by promoting inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression, NO
cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and IL-681,83.
Recent studies showed that DJ-1 is highly expressed in astrocytes and may alter the expression
of genes involved in antioxidant defence and neuroinflammatory processes74,79. Astrocyte-derived factors,
such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), are involved in the mechanism
of astrocyte-mediated protection and were decreased in DJ-1 KO mice81. Decreased TNFα expression and
secretion may decrease astrocytic support of neuronal viability during early stages of neurodegeneration.
Astrocyte-derived TNFα is essential for maintaining synaptic connectivity and plasticity. PGE2 is released
from astrocytes following glutamate activation and is likely a cytoprotective event. Therefore, decreased
16

astrocytic release of TNFα and PGE2 induced by DJ-1 KO may decrease neuronal viability81,81.
In studies conducted upon LPS treatment, DJ-1 interacts with signal transducer and activator of
transcription 1 (STAT1) implicated in neuroinflammation, augmenting STAT1 activation by impairing the
interaction between STAT1 and its phosphatase SHP-1 in astrocytes and microglia23,80. DJ-1 deficiency
affected microglial central metabolism and their ability to degrade α-syn via autophagy23,75. Furthermore,
some authors proposed that DJ-1 reduces the release of microglial inflammatory mediators by promoting
the astrocytic expression of prostaglandin D2 synthase (PTGDS) and production of prostaglandin D2
(PGD2)79,81.
It is important to note that even though DJ-1 mutations are rare causes of early-onset PD, the
impact of DJ-1 dysfunction to PD pathogenesis remains to be widely studied. Further understanding of
the pathways in which DJ-1 is involved is crucial to unravelling the complex mechanisms underlying PD
and may offer potential targets for therapeutic interventions in the future.
1.2.2 DJ-1 Knockout models
DJ-1 Knockout models (KO) mouse models provide an important tool in PD investigation, allowing
for the study of PD with a genetic background, differently for what is observed in 6-OHDA and MPTP
models that rely solely on oxidative stress mechanisms limited to the nigrostriatal pathway74,76,77,81,84. Table
3 contains a summary of the phenotypes described for DJ-1 KO mouse models.
Behavioral tests performed in DJ-1 KO mouse models have shown subtle behavioral
abnormalities73,85. Regarding locomotor activity, assessed with the open field paradigm, mice showed a
marked reduction in horizontal and vertical activity73,85. A study evaluating motor performance in the
rotarod, open field, and grip-strength showed that DJ-1 KO mice had impaired gait and motor features86.
In the adhesive removal test, known to assess somatosensory abilities, DJ-1 KO animals at 2-4 and 7
months old had a decreased performance compared to Wild-type controls87. However, at the age of 18
months no significant differences were found, likely because of the age-dependent decline of Wild-type
(WT) animals87. In contrast, some studies showed no significant motor alterations in similar paradigms
proposing that DJ-1 KO mice display only a slight motor defect22,65,76.
Regarding non-motor behavior, few studies have addressed these alterations in DJ-1 KO animals.
In the open field test, mice spent less time moving and rearing and had fewer instances of stereotyped
behavior, indicating a possible anxious phenotype73. In the appetitive instrumental learning, used to assess
altered striatal function, DJ-1 KO rats displayed similar behavior as WT controls88. In several studies DJ-1
KO mice seem to fail to recapitulate the major clinical features of PD22,76,81. An explanation for this, proposed
17

by some authors, is the existence of compensatory mechanisms in mice that may protect them from
neurodegeneration and consequent establishment of motor symptoms81.
Studies performed in DJ-1 KO rodents have shown their increased susceptibility to neuronal death
due to oxidative stress in MPTP, 6-OHDA and hydrogen peroxide models65,77,85. On the other hand, over-
expression of DJ-1 seems to protect cells from several oxidative stimuli85. Immunohistochemical analysis
showed no major differences in the number of TH-positive neurons in the striatum nor SNpc73,74,80,85.
Neurochemical analysis showed that KO mice present increased dopamine uptake, reduced evoked
dopamine release, decreased autoinhibitory effects of the D2 receptor, reduced postsynaptic dopamine
signaling and impaired cortical striatal long-term depression23. Decreased levels of synaptophysin, a
presynaptic marker, were also detected in DJ-1-deficient mouse, which may be indicative of impaired
synaptic network89. This suggests that dopaminergic neurons may not be degenerated, but they are not
fully functional in DJ-1 KO mice.

2. RESEARCH OBJECTIVES
The LC has been described to be related to the development of non-motor symptoms clinically
associated with PD. DSP-4 is a neurotoxin, specific to the LC, that has been widely used to understand
the role of LC degeneration in neurodegenerative disorders. DJ-1 KO mouse models have been shown to
present age dependent progression, of both motor and non-motor behavior. It is important to note that
PD is a multifactorial disease, and it is likely that a combination of genetic and environmental factors
contribute to its development. Therefore, studies that combine different types of mouse models, such as
DJ-1 KO mice with other genetic or environmental manipulations, may provide a more complete
understanding of the pathogenesis of the disease.
The main goal of this work was to develop a PD mouse model able to recapitulate noradrenergic
degeneration in the LC in the context of a genetic PD form (DJ-1 mutation). For that, we have established
the following specific objectives:
(1) Adjusting the appropriate DSP-4 dosage to elicit noradrenergic degeneration with a functional
impact on a DJ-1 KO mouse model.
(2) Characterize motor and non-motor behavioral alterations to understand the effect of DSP-4
administration in WT and DJ-1 animals.
(3) Characterize the histological alterations in the LC and nigrostriatal pathway to understand the
effect of DSP-4 administration in WT and DJ-1 animals.
20

3. MATERIALS AND METHODS
3.1 Animals
In this study, we used a DJ-1 KO (DJ-1-/-) mouse model established by Goldberg et al 87. An
heterozygous DJ-1 (+/-) male and female mice were kindly provided by his lab (Center for Neurologic
Diseases, Harvard Medical School, Boston) where this mouse model was originally established. From this
couple, we have established a colony at ICVS by crossing heterozygous DJ-1 (+/-) male and female mice
and performing genotyping of the offspring.
10 to 17 weeks old DJ-1 mice and WT littermates, male and female, were used under the scope
of the project 008-2022, according to the Portuguese national authority for animal research, Direção
Geral de Alimentação e Veterinária (DGAV) and Ethical Subcommittee in Life and Health Sciences (SECVS;
ID: SECVS 142/2016, University of Minho). Animals were housed in groups, obeying animals’ limit per
cage, in a temperature and humidity-controlled room, on 12h light/dark cycles, and access to food and
water ad libitum.
3.2 DSP-4 Administration
DSP-4 (C8417, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was systemically injected by intraperitoneal injection
(i.p.) at the defined dosage (table 4). A set of animals received the same volume of saline (NaCl), which

3.3 Behavioral Tests
21

Two weeks after DSP-4 injection, animals went through behavioral tests covering both motor and
non-motor features. The battery of tests chosen covered several symptoms associated with PD, including
depression, anxiety, cognitive impairments, impaired locomotor activity and gait dysfunction. Prior to each
behavioral test, all mice were habituated to the testing room for 30 min. The experimental timeline is
described in figure 5.
Figure 5. Experimental timeline. First, animals were separated into groups and injected with either saline or DSP-4. During
the 2 weeks following injection animals were weighed and welfare was assessed every 2 days. Then, behavioral assessment
was started with tests evaluating anxiety and depressive-like behavior: Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) , Open Field (OF), Marble
Burying (MB), Sucrose Splash Test (SST), Forced Swimming Test (FST). On the following two weeks we evaluated memory
and learning paradigms: Novel Object Recognition (NOR) and Morris Water Maze (MWM). On the fourth week we assessed
motor coordination and balance: Beam Balance, Motor Swimming Test (MST) and Footprint Analysis. At week 5, we evaluated
memory and learning using the Contextual Fear Conditioning (CFC).
3.3.1 Elevated Plus Maze
EPM is often used to evaluate anxiety-like behavior in rodents91. This test uses mice’s innate
tendency to explore new environments and their natural tendency to avoid bright, elevated places42. The
test was carried out under a bright white light in an apparatus consisting of a black propylene plus-shaped
platform elevated 74.4cm above the floor with two open arms (50.8cm) and two closed arms (10.2 cm).
Animals were placed individually in the center of the platform and all sessions were video recorded over
5min and manually analyzed using the Kinoscope software (V.3.0.4, SourceForge, San Diego, CA, USA).
The time animals spent in each arm and the number of entries in each arm were used as measures of
anxiety-like behavior.
3.3.2 Open Field
OF can be used to assess anxiety-like behavior91. As anxious animals tend to avoid open areas,
anxious behavior can be evaluated using the time mice spend in the center of the box and the time spent
on the periphery of the box43,91. Animals were placed in the center of an illuminated square arena (43.2cm
x 43.2cm) enclosed by a 30.5cm high wall. Movements were tracked for 5 min, using a 16-beam infrared
system (MedAssociates Inc., Twin City, GA, USA), and data was analyzed using the Activity Monitor
22

software (MedAssociates Inc.). Distance traveled and time spent at the center versus periphery were
considered as a measure of anxiety-like behavior.
3.3.3 Marble-Burying
Marble-burying is frequently used to address anxiety related to novelty and obsessive-compulsive
behavior91. The burying of the marble is a natural defense mechanism that follows anxiety or a condition
of stress42. When a strange object is placed inside the cage, mice usually start to display specific behavior,
such as digging, burrowing, burying and grooming43. Each mouse was placed in a cage with six marbles
for 30min. The number of marbles buried was used as an index to evaluate anxiety-like behavior. Marble
was considered buried if at least two-thirds were hidden in the bedding.
3.3.4 Sucrose Splash Test
Rodents developing depressive-like behavior usually spend less time grooming and are more
immobile42,91. For this test, animals were placed in a clean empty cage. A 10% sucrose solution was
sprayed on the dorsal coat of mice. The sucrose solution is usually chosen as a releaser and an enduring
factor of grooming due to its viscosity92. All sessions were video recorded over 5min and analyzed manually
using the Kinoscope software. The latency time to initiate a grooming, time spent grooming and number
of grooming initiated were used as an index to measure depressive-like behavior.
3.3.5 Forced Swimming Test
FST evaluates helplessness or depressive-like behavior91. This test was carried out under the
concept that rodents show increased periods of inertia when they learn that escaping is not possible.
Each animal was individually placed in glass cylinders filled with water (30cm of depth; 22ºC). All sessions
were video recorded during 7 min total and the first 2 min were considered habituation period. Each
session was analyzed manually using the Kinoscope software. The immobility time (IT), or total time spent
immobile, was measured. Mice were considered immobile when remaining passively floating or doing
minimal movements needed to maintain the head above the surface.
3.3.6 Novel Object Recognition Test
This test makes advantage of rodent’s natural tendency for novelty to investigate several aspects
of learning and memory45. It requires a habituation period of 3 days, in which mice were placed in a white
empty box for 15 minutes. On the first test day, two identical objects were placed in the corners of the
23

box. 24h later, two other identical objects were placed in the same spot on the boxes. On the same day,
mice had a training session, where two identical novel objects were placed in the box. 1h later, the rodent
was returned to the same arena, which contained one familiar object and a novel object. During each
session animals were left to explore for 10 min and all sessions were video recorded. Given its preference
for novelty, if the mouse can recognize the familiar object, it will spend more time around the novel
object93. All sessions were video recorded and analyzed manually using the Kinoscope software. The time
the animal spends with the familiar object is compared to the time spent with the novel object and used
as an index to evaluate learning and memory.
3.3.7 Morris Water Maze Test
Morris Water Maze is the most used test to evaluate learning and memory, specifically spatial
memory acquisition91. The test was performed in a circular swimming pool (150cm diameter, 50cm
depth, 22ºC), divided into four quadrants, with a mobile target platform set about 1-1.5cm under water
surface. During four consecutive days, each animal had four trials, each one starting in a different
quadrant. They were given 1min maximum to find the platform. The time spent and the travelled distance
to reach the platform was used as a measure of hippocampal-dependent learning. The average speed in
each session was used to assess if the results were correlated with motor impairments. All sessions were
video recorded and analyzed using EthoVision XT 13 software (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands).
3.3.8 Balance Beam Test
The Beam test is designed to assess motor coordination deficits and is one of the most used
tests in PD modeling91. Mice were placed in an elevated, open platform, connected by a beam to a closed
box. Mice were trained for 3 days on a square beam with 12 mm of diameter. On day 4, animals were
tested on a large square beam (12mm), on a round large beam (17mm) and on a thin square beam
(11mm). The time animals took to cross each beam was registered. Whenever the animal stopped, time
was discounted. If the animal fell or turned around it was considered a failed trial. Each animal had two
opportunities to fail, otherwise it was not included in the analysis.
3.3.9 Motor Swimming Test
MST is the most frequently used task to evaluate motor impairments and deficits in PD models91.
Animals were trained for two consecutive days (three trials per animal) to traverse a clear Perspex water
tank (100 cm long, 22º C) to a safe black platform at the end. Mice were tested for three consecutive
24

days (two trials per animal), and the latency to cross the water was measured from the 60cm distance
mark.
3.3.10 Footprint Analysis
Several different parameters of gait, such as enlargement of base width, can be analyzed using
footprint patterns94. Mice’s hind and forepaws were coated with black or red non-toxic paints, respectively,
before walking along an inclined corridor (100cm x 4.2cm x 10cm), containing a clean paper sheet on
the bottom. The stride width was obtained by manually measuring the distance between two pawprints.
Three values were measured for six consecutive steps and the mean of three values was used.
3.3.11 Contextual Fear Conditioning
Contextual Fear Conditioning is used to assess learning and memory formation, usually in relation
to fear responses and the formation of fear memories95. In this task, mice are placed in a novel context
and an aversive stimulus is provided43. When animals are returned to the same context, if they can
remember and associate that environment with the aversive stimuli they were given before, they will
demonstrate a freezing response. Freezing can be defined as “absence of movement except for
respiration” in response to fear. The presence or absence of a freezing response, as well as the time
animal’s take to respond, is used as criteria for learning. The protocol herein employed used a light as a
cue. 20s after the light turned on, animals received a 2s shock (0.7V), in 3min intervals, during 9min
total. On the second day, animals were subjected to the same context, and when the light turned on, the
responsiveness of mice, freezing, could be used as an index of contextual fear conditioning. 2h later, on
the second day, animals were placed in a different context and the responsiveness was analyzed. All
sessions were video recorded and analyzed manually using the Kinoscope software. It is important to
note than some mouse strains require repeated exposures to the repetitive stimuli in other to retrieve fear
memories and thus, this test often needs further optimization40,95.
3.4 Histological Analysis
After behavioral assessment, mice were anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine (150 mg/kg) and
medetomidine (0.3 mg/Kg) and were transcardially perfused with cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in
phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Brains were collected and transferred to a tube containing 4% PFA in
PBS. After 48h of incubation at 4ºC brains were transferred to a solution of 30% sucrose in PBS containing
0.01% of sodium azide and kept at 4ºC until being processed. Brains were sectioned coronally using a
25

vibratome (VT1000S, Leica, Germany) at thickness of 40 µm.
3.4.1 TH+ free-floating immunohistochemistry
TH catalyzes the initial and rate-limiting step in the biosynthesis of catecholamines (i.e., dopamine
and noradrenaline). TH-containing catecholamine neurons are specifically affected on PD and hence TH
is used as a marker to assess dopaminergic neuronal cell loss in animal models for PD.
Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is widely used to target specific antigens within tissue. Performing TH+ IHC
allows to selectively mark dopaminergic and noradrenergic neurons and thus analyze DA and NA neurons
preservation and/or degeneration. Free-floating immunohistochemistry is a useful method when we
intend to see certain structures, like dendrites and axons given that the staining is performed uniformly
from all section angles within the tissue.
Six series of coronal sections of striatum, SNpc and LC were selected for free-floating
immunohistochemistry. Sections were initially treated with PBS 3% HO for 20 min to block endogenous
2 2
peroxidase activity, permeabilized in 0.3% PBS-Triton (PBS-T) for 10 min and then blocked in PBS with
10% fetal calf serum (FCS) for 1h to prevent non-specific binding. Then, sections were incubated in PBS
with 2% FCS anti-TH antibody (rabbit polyclonal IgG, 1:1000; Merck Millipore) overnight at 4°C. On the
next day, brain sections were incubated during 30 min at RT with a biotinylated secondary antibody (goat
anti-polyvalent, TP-125-BN, ThermoFisher Scientific) and then during 30 min with the streptavidin-
peroxidase solution (TP-125-HR, ThermoFisher Scientific). To detect the streptavidin-biotin
immunoenzymatic complex, a substrate was given to this reaction, using 12.5 mg of 3,3’-
diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB; D5905, Sigma) dissolved in 25 ml of Tris-HCl 0.05 M
(pH=7.6) with 62.5 µl of HO. Sections were then mounted on superfrost slides and air dried for 24h at
2 2
RT before analysis.
3.4.2 TH+ fibers density quantification
TH+ fibers in the striatum were imaged using brightfield illumination (SZX16, Olympus) and the
optical density was measured using the ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, v1.48, USA). Each
image was first converted to 8-bit format. Then, using the regions of interest (ROI) manager, the dorsal
striatal area was selected in both hemispheres, as well as three different background regions next to the
striatal area. White and black values were inverted (black=0, white=255) and the selected settings for the
analysis were area and mean grey value (average gray value within the selection). After that, the
measurement was performed, and the values for striatal area and backgrounds were saved.
26

3.4.3 TH+ cells quantification
TH-positive cells in the SNpc and LC were visualized and counted using a bright‐field microscope
(BX51; Olympus, Center Valley, PA) equipped with a digital camera (PixeLINK PL‐A622, CANIMPEX
Enterprises, Halifax, NS, Canada). Using the Visiomorph software (V2.12.3.0; Visiopharm, Hørsholm,
Denmark) the boundaries of the SNpc and LC area were drawn and total TH+ cells were counted in both
hemispheres.
3.5 Statistical Analysis
Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk. To evaluate the homogeneity of variance
a Levene’s test was used. For datasets that were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests (Kruskal-
Wallis) or (Mann-Whitney) were used. Post-hoc testing using pairwise comparisons were used for
parametric (Turkey’s correction) and non-parametric (Dunn’s correction) tests.
Data regarding histological assessment and non-motor behavior were analyzed using one-way
ANOVA to compare the four groups. Data regarding motor behavior behavioral assessment were analyzed
using ANCOVA to compare the mean values for the groups taking account animal’s weight. When
comparing two independent groups, independent sample t-tests were used.
The significance value was set as 0.05 for all statistical tests and all the results are presented as
mean ± SD (standard deviation). JASP (version 0.17.1; Amsterdam, The Netherlands) was used as a
statistical software platform used for all statistical analysis and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0; GraphPad
Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used to perform graphical representation.

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
In this work we aimed to develop a PD mouse model able to recapitulate LC-noradrenergic
degeneration in the context of DJ-1 mutation. By administering DSP-4 in DJ-1 KO mice we expected to
lesion LC-NA neurons. Through a battery of tests covering motor coordination, locomotion, cognitive
functions and the presence/absence of depressive or anxious behavior we expected to understand the
impact of noradrenergic degeneration in the progression and aggravation of PD symptoms.
4.1 Adjusting the appropriate DSP-4 dosage
Given the increased susceptibility of DJ-1 KO animals to other toxins (e.g., 6-OHDA and MPTP)
described in several studies, we first addressed whether higher dosages of DSP-4 would significantly
compromise animal’s welfare65,83,88. We did a preliminary study in a small number of animals to test three
different dosages previously described in the literature for WT animals, 10mg/kg (n=6), 25mg/kg (n=5),
and 50mg/kg (n=7) in DJ-1 KO animals herein used68,70,71.

We also performed a preliminary assessment of the impact of these DSP-4 dosages on animals’
behaviour (figure 7). Previous studies have already showed that lower dosages (10 and 25mg/kg)
produce a partial lesion, with no apparent influence on behavior, while higher dosages (50mg/kg) have
a greater impact and can directly affect behavior14,55,60,64. Our results revealed no major impairments at the
motor (figures 7a and b) nor at the non-motor level (figures 7b,c,d,e and f) in 10 mg/kg and 25
mg/kg DSP-4 compared to 50mg/kg DSP-4 injected DJ-1 animals. Moreover, the 50mg/kg DSP-4
injected animals have shown less discrepancies in the results, contrary to 10 and 25 mg/kg DSP-4
injected animals that presented increased variability within results. This suggests a better-established
effect of the 50mg/kg DSP-4 dosage on behavior. Therefore, we decided to proceed the behavioral
assessment only with the highest dosage (50mg/kg), as it did not significantly compromise animals’
welfare and was more likely to have an impact at the behavioral level.
Even though we acknowledge the relevance of assessing the histological outcome of the lowest
dosages to have a dose-response curve, this assessment would require the use of a lot of animals which
is not in line with the 3 ’s principle97. Specifically, it would not be in line with the reduction principle that
defends the use of fewer animals to answer the research question. Herein, we reduced the number of
animals used by administering only the highest dosage. Moreover, we maximized the information
obtained per animals by performing a complex battery of behavioral tests and performing histological
analysis on these animals, avoiding the subsequent use of additional animals, without compromising
animal welfare.
4.2 Behavioral effects of DSP-4 administration in DJ-1 KO animals.
Once we established the suitable dosage of DSP-4 to be administered, we proceeded to assess the
effects of noradrenergic degeneration in both motor and non-motor behavior in WT and DJ-1 KO mice.
4.2.1 Alterations on Motor Behavior
Most PD models mostly rely on the evaluation of motor symptoms1,91. We performed commonly
used behavioral tests used in current PD models to evaluate motor performance (motor swimming test),
balance (beam balance), and gait dysfunction (footprint analysis)91. This outcome is not only important to
understand if the model has some motor impairments, but also to understand if the results obtained in
some non-motor test (e.g., Morris Water Maze) could be influenced by any underlying motor alteration.
In the Beam Balance test, mice were trained to travel through a beam, and, on the test day, the
time took to reach the safe platform was used to assess motor function. We conducted the test day on
30

three beams, each one with a different difficulty level. Our results did not reveal any statistically significant
differences between groups, even controlling for a possible influence of animals’ weight on their latency
to transverse each beam. Nevertheless, there are some tendencies worth to further explore (figure 8a).
On the 12mm beam (F(3,25)=1.184, p=0.3358), the easiest to cross, WT vehicle and DJ-1 DSP-4
injected animals had similar latencies. On the 17mm (F(3, 28)=0.6727, p=0.5760) and 11mm beam
(F(1,19)=0.078, p=0.783), there is a slightly higher latency of WT animals injected with DSP-4 in
comparison to vehicle WT animals. Despite the great variability, on the 11mm beam, which is the hardest
beam, WT and DJ-1 animals injected with DSP-4 show a higher latency than their vehicles. Moreover, the
latency of the DJ-1 KO animals with DSP-4 seems to be higher than injected WT animals. Therefore, the
absence of DJ-1 might increase the impact of DSP-4 effects on animals’ balance.

MST evaluates swimming abilities, which require fine motor coordination of the limbs91. Then
again, despite the lower dispersion of these results (figure 8b), the slight differences observed between
groups do not reach statistical significance (F(1,23)=1.657, p=0.211). Still, there is a subtle increase of
DJ-1 animals’ latency in comparison to T mice, that is not altered by DSP-4 administration (figure 8b).
A similar tendency is observed in the footprint test (F(1,24)=0.252, p=0.620) (figure 8c). DJ-1 animals
show an increased base width when compared to WT animals, but DSP-4 injected animals did not
presented differences compared to vehicle groups. This gait dysfunction is a typical feature in PD patients
and has been previously described in DJ-1 rats86,91,94.
Despite the lack of significance, the DSP-4 administration seems to have an impact in animal’s
balance, which is more pronounced in DJ-1 animals. However, in the swimming ability and gait
dysfunction, the DJ-1 mutation seems to be the most prominent factor that is affecting the results.
Therefore, it appears that DJ-1 animals develop a minor motor phenotype that can be exacerbated with
the DSP-4 administration. The lack of statistical significance could be attributed to the considerable
variability within the groups, which might require a larger sample size for conclusive findings.
In order to understand our model, it is important to compare our results with other previously
described models. 6-OHDA, for instance, often reflects an end-stage of the disease with robust behavioral
phenotypes54. Even though some studies performed partial lesion models as an attempt to mimic an
earlier PD stage, the behavioral readouts are less consistent, and it is still not possible to reproduce earlier
PD events4,91,94. The administration of DSP-4 in combination with a 6-OHDA lesion led to an aggravation of
typical symptomatology displayed by these toxin models (i.e., forelimb akinesia)62. MPTP models usually
are good at inducing parkinsonism and different dosages are being optimized in order to recapitulate
several stages of the disease4,91. When administered in MPTP models, DSP-4 led to a marked activity
decrease, suggesting the establishment of a severe motor phenotype58,65. DJ-1 genetic models have shown
an age-dependent progression associated with mild motor behavior impairment73,90. Our results show that
DJ-1 animals have slightly worsened performance on balance, swimming and an aggravated gait
dysfunction. The administration of DSP-4 on DJ-1 animals led to an increased impairment on motor
balance but did not seem to affect swimming ability or gait functioning. Thus, our results point that the
combination of DSP-4 and DJ-1 may be able to mimic mild motor impairments, usually displayed by
patients in the prodromal stage, in contrast to 6-OHDA and MPTP models that best mimic the
establishment of the most prominent motor symptoms in the clinical stage.
Overall, in the motor assessment herein performed, the combination of knocking out DJ-1 and
DSP-4 administration only seemed to have mild effect on animals’ balance and did not exacerbate the
32

effects observed in swimming and gait analysis. In earlier stages, patients usually start to manifest slight
motor alterations that are not noticeable at first, like postural instability, balance problems and difficulty
in fine motor movements3,4,98. At this point, nigrostriatal degeneration begins. Moreover, the NA depletion
might potentiate degeneration processes, as the LC projects to the nigrostriatal areas and also to the
spinal cord and can be influencing fine motor movements8,18. In future studies, to better understand the
effects of DSP-4 administration combined with the DJ-1 KO phenotype, it would be interesting to perform
additional motor behavioral tests, including the grip-strength, used to assess skeletal muscle function,
the pole test used assess basal ganglia related movement disorders and the adhesive removal test, used
to evaluate sensory and motor deficits99,100.
4.2.2 Alterations on Cognition
In PD patients, cognitive deficits are present both as a prodromal feature and as a long-term
complication in later stages of the disease6,18. Learning and memory formation involves hippocampal
context-dependent associative learning, declarative and working memory, and amygdala-mediated
affective learning, which have been described to be influenced by LC-NA system6,27,44. Distinct modulations
can have different and opposite functional roles in mediating learning and responses to aversive stimuli26,31.
To address the impact of LC lesion and DJ-1 KO mutation on these responses, we selected some standard
behavioral tests to assess spatial memory (Morris Water Maze), learning, and memory (Novel Object
Recognition and Contextual Fear Conditioning).
The MWM is commonly used to evaluate learning and memory, specifically spatial memory
acquisition91. The distance traveled and latency to reach the platform are used to evaluate memory
learning. Over sessions, healthy animals usually travel lower distances and require less time to reach the
platform, indicating memory retrieval101. Our analysis on the fourth and last MWM day showed no
significant differences between groups in the latency to reach the platform (F(3,15)=10.860, p=0.895)
(figure 9a). Regarding distance traveled to reach the platform, there were significant differences among
groups (F(3,16)=4.695, p=0.015). Post hoc testing using pairwise comparisons of the estimated marginal
means with Turkey’s correction revealed a significant difference between WT DSP-4 (mean=677.996,
SD=142.369) and DJ-1 DSP-4 (mean=337.978, SD=141.465, p<0.05) mice, suggesting that DJ-1 DSP-
4 treated animals have a better spatial memory acquisition ability than DSP-4 injected WT mice. In a
study conducted using DSP-4 in paraquat and maneb-induced mouse PD model, DSP-4 led to impaired
spatial learning ability in the MWM27. Another study also showed that, in the same test, when combined
with MPTP, DSP-4 administration also impaired spatial learning14. Moreover, another study showed that
33

the combination of DSP-4 with 6-OHDA affected working memory in the Radial Maze test102. However, in
the same studies, when administered alone, DSP-4 lesions did not affected working memory. This is in
line with our results since WT DSP-4 lesioned animals did not show impaired spatial memory acquisition.
This suggests that NA depletion itself is not sufficient to evoke cognitive deterioration but can act as a
potentiator when combined with other toxins that elicit neurodegeneration. However, our results show
conflicting evidence, since when administered in DJ-1 mice, DSP-4 seems to improve spatial memory
acquisition.
The differences observed in MWM tests sometimes are caused by locomotor deficits. To assess
this, we analyzed animals’ speed. This analysis showed significant differences among groups (F(3,
11)=10.06, p<0.01). Post hoc testing using Turkey’s correction revealed significant difference between
DJ-1 DSP-4 (mean=15.41, SD=3.519) and WT DSP-4 (mean= 31.66, SD= 5.699, p<0.05) and DJ-1
Vehicle (mean=31.72, SD=6.548, p<0.01). DJ-1 DSP-4 animals showed significantly lower swimming
speed than DJ-1 vehicle and WT DSP-4. This suggests an impairment in motor functioning of DJ-1 DSP-
4 treated animals rather than a spatial memory acquisition deficit. Combined with the tendencies
observed in motor behavior testing described earlier, this further supports the hypothesis that combining
a lesion in the LC with DJ-1 KO mutation leads to mild motor deficits.
The LC has been described to have global function in mediating arousal to strongly aversive
stimuli and to play a critical role in threat-related learning8,18. Threatening cues or events leads to a
heightened vigilance state and prepares a rapid flexible behavioral response that often translates to mice
as the presence of a freezing state95. CFC is used to assess learning and memory formation, usually in
relation to fear responses and the formation of fear memories91,95. A study conducted with DSP-4 has
shown that injected animals show impaired learning and memory in a similar paradigm68. Other authors
defend that LC lesions seem to increase fear extinction71. In our test, mice were exposed to a fear stressor
and were later returned to that same context and to a novel one, completely different from the box where
the aversive stimulus was given. Our results showed no significant differences in the traumatic context
(F(3,14) = 2.210, p=0.132) nor in the novel context (H=1.687,df=3, p=0.640)(figure 9b). Still, some
tendencies are worth exploring. In our work, WT animals had similar freezing percentages in both
contexts, suggesting that these animals did not discriminate potentially threatening contexts. WT DSP-4
injected animals had a similar performance as WT Vehicle animals in both contexts. An explanation for
this may be than some mouse strains require repeated exposures to the repetitive stimuli in other to
recall fear memories40,95. DJ-1 DSP-4 mice spent more time in freezing behavior, suggesting the retrieval
of fear memory. When placed in a novel context, both DJ-1 groups spent less time immobile, suggesting
34

the fear response was not triggered. Herein, our results suggest that the administration of DSP-4 alone
is not sufficient to affect the retrieval of fear memory, but DJ-1 animals, vehicle and DSP-4 injected, both
shown improved memory retrieval.
Results regarding the NOR test are not represented as the WT vehicle group did not discriminate
against the objects used (Annex I, figure 1a). Therefore, the results are not suitable for analysis. This
test needs further optimization since different strains of mice may have different object perceptions45,93.

In future studies, using a combination of different tests should be considered. For instance, even
though the MWM is a well-established and validated test, mice may get stressed in a water context, which
35

may influence the results. Alternatively, we could use a Barnes Maze test which evaluates spatial memory
by measuring the time taken by mice to find a target hole on a circular platform using visual cues or a
Radial Arm Maze assesses spatial memory as mice navigate through a maze with arms extending from
a central hub99. Regarding the evaluation of fear memory, the Passive Avoidance test can also be
considered43,91. In this test, mice learn to avoid an aversive compartment and display a preference for a
safe compartment. This allows us to assess long-term memory and, unlike the CFC, does not require
moving the animals between rooms and diminishes handling, which can act as stressors and influence
the test readout. Regarding the NOR test, besides optimizing the protocol for this specific strain, we could
use complementary tests in order to understand the formation and retrieval of short and long-memory in
this model. The Social Recognition test, for instance, does not evaluate object recognition but rather
evaluates mice’s ability to recognize and remember other individuals91,95.
4.2.3 Alterations on Depressive and Anxious-like Behavior
Depression and anxiety are common in earlier stages, prior to motor alterations, and are one of
the most frequent symptoms among PD patients (25-40%)24,33,34. Some studies have linked increased LC
activity and the development of anxiety related disorders41,72. In the context of depression, some studies
showed a correlation between the decline of NA content and the reduction of LC pigmentation with the
prevalence of depression in PD patients6,34. Herein, we selected tests to evaluate both anxiety-like (Open
Field, Elevated Plus Maze and Marble-burying), and depressive-like behavior (Forced Swimming Test and
Sucrose Splash Test).
The EPM and the OF tests use mice’s innate tendency to explore new environments and their
natural tendency to avoid bright, open places42. Some authors reported that, in the same tests, LC lesioned
rats had reduced anxious behavior6. However, other studies showed that, in mice, NA depletion alone is
not sufficient to significantly affect the exploration of open contexts14. Our results showed that, in the EPM
test, there were no significant differences (F(3,23)=0.181, p=0.908) (figure 10a). Both DSP-4 treated
groups have shown a small tendency to spend more time in the open arms, suggesting a reduced anxiety-
behavior. However, the differences between the groups were almost unnoticeable. In the OF no significant
differences were found (F(3,16)=1.471, p=0.260) (figure10b). DSP-4 treated animals spend less time
in the centre of the box than the respective controls. This suggests that the administration of DSP-4 may
lead to the development of anxiety-like behavior. DJ-1 animals spend more time in the center than WT
vehicles, which indicates that the DJ-1 mutation did not lead to anxious-like behavior. However, these
results may be related to the test itself since both OF and EPM tests readouts can often be influenced by
36

external factors, thus requiring to be careful performed and analysed. In the future, other tests may be
considered to complement the assessment of anxious behavior. For instance, the light and dark box that
test takes advantage of the innate aversion of mice to brightly lit, open spaces and their preference for
dark, enclosed spaces, or the hole-board test, which is based on the natural tendency of rodents to explore
new environments and the conflict between their exploratory drive and fear of open spaces42,103.

Marble-burying is frequently used to address anxiety related to novelty91. When a unfamiliar object
is placed in the cage, mice usually start displaying specific behavior, such as burying, indicators of anxious
behavior43. Previous studies report that DSP-4 ablation in rats induces anxiety-like phenotypes in response
37

to novelty stress6,67. Studies using DSP-4 in mice have also shown increased anxiety-like behavior specific
to novelty in the marble burying test50. Figure 10c shows that both DJ-1 vehicle, WT and DJ-1 DSP-4
treated animals buried more marbles than WT vehicles, although these differences did not reach statistical
significance (H=1.923, df=3, p=0.588). Herein, both DSP-4 and DJ-1 mutation seem to have an impact
on the development of anxiety related to novelty. Still, the combination of DJ-1 KO and DSP-4 led to a
small increase in this phenotype, with less discrepant results, suggesting the establishment of anxious-
like behavior on these animals.
The FST evaluates helplessness or depressive-like behavior under the concept that rodents show
increased periods of inertia when they learn that escape is not possible91. In studies where NA was
depleted, mice showed an increased immobility time in the FST compared to control animals14. Our results
show contrary effects of DSP-4, without reaching statistical significance (F(3,23)=1.178), p=0.340)
(figure 10d). In WT animals, the administration of DSP-4 led to a decrease in immobility time while in
DJ-1 animals DSP-4 led to an increased immobility time. However, WT vehicle mice spend more time
immobile, suggesting that these animals may have developed a depressive-like behavior that is not
dependent on DSP-4 nor DJ-1 mutation. This can also indicate that the test was not well performed, or,
since this test is a stressful event for mice, we should consider that this may not be the most suitable
approach to assess helplessness. In the future, an alternative for the FST could be the Tail Suspension
test which is based on the notion that the animal will try to escape from the stressful situation (being
hanging by the tail).
Rodents developing depressive-like symptoms usually spend less time grooming and are more
immobile42,91. The SST enhances the need for grooming by splashing animals’ coats with a sucrose
solution. While DJ-1 and WT vehicle groups showed similar results, DSP-4 treated WT animals have spent
less time grooming, correlated with the establishment of depressive-like behavior (F(3,23)=1.517,
p=0.237) (figure 10e). DJ-1 DSP-4 injected animals shown a reduced depressive-like behavior
compared to WT DSP-4 animals. Herein, although DSP-4 lesions led to an increased depressive-like
behavior in WT mice, DJ-1 mice do not seem to be affected. In another task (Sucrose Preference test)
used to assess anhedonia, the inability or reduced ability to experience pleasure or enjoyment, depleting
NA is not sufficient to induce anhedonic behavior in mice but, when combined with DA degeneration, a
significant depressive-like phenotype was noticed14. In the future, a good option to complement our
behavioral characterization could be the sucrose preference test, which measures the level of depression
(or anhedonic state) by the preference for a sucrose (sweet) solution rather than water103.
38

Assessing anxiety and depressive-like behavior in mice has several limitations, as mice are
anxious by nature34,42,43. We tried to diminish at the most external factors that may interfere with the
outcome of the tests. For instance, mice were handled regularly since the beginning of the experiments,
all anxiety related tests were performed at lowest affluence periods in the animal facility, lights were
always turned off and animals were left alone in the room while tests were recorded. Still, the
discrepancies obtained indicate that these tests were not successfully performed or that our model does
not develop an anxious nor depressive-like phenotype. The lack of statistical significance can mostly be
related to the variability between animals. Further studies using a higher number of animals, in order to
minimize variability, are a first improvement to assess these alterations. Moreover, there are several
variability factors to be considered. For instance, the fact that we used three separated sets of animals to
perform the tests may be influencing the outcome. This can be related to several external factors such
animals’ stress, noise in the facilities and the user itself. Second, different ages and genders could be
correlated with different outcomes. To overcome these factors, for every set of animals we always tried
to diminish discrepancies, as in every set we tried to use same number of males and females, at similar
ages, between different experimental groups. Moreover, we have separated each experimental group of
animals per age and gender and performed statistical analysis for each case (Annex I, figures 1-4). As
we did not find any differences, we proceeded to analyse all data together. We acknowledge that having
one single set of animals with a higher number of animals per group, with the same gender and age, may
had contributes to diminish discrepancies. However, we could not predict if the animals would develop
any phenotype or have any complication during the experimental timeline, which would lead to a
significant loss of animals. Moreover, having more animals in one single set would require generating
more animals that would not be used, which would not be in line with the 3 ’s principle.
4.3 Histological Alterations
In order to understand if the readouts obtained in the behavioral analysis can be a consequence
of noradrenergic and/or nigrostriatal degeneration, we performed histological analysis on the LC, striatum
and SNpc.
4.3.1 Effect of DSP-4 on Locus Coeruleus
DSP-4 is a highly selective drug for LC neurons60. Hence, it was of interest to evaluate the level
of degeneration provoked by DSP-4 administration in the LC. The majority of the LC (90%) is composed
of noradrenergic neurons31. Ideally, the level of noradrenergic degeneration would be addressed using
39

DBH immunohistochemistry, which is specific to noradrenergic neurons14,70,104. However, after several trials
to optimize the protocol, we were not successful in using DBH antibody (PA5-34664, ThermoFisher
Scientific) in immunohistochemistry. We could overcome this by using DBH antibody in
immunofluorescence24,50. However, this would not allow a rapid visualization and quantification of the
neurons as it would require longer image acquisition in a confocal microscope. Alternatively, TH staining
through immunohistochemistry is fully optimized in our lab and can also be used to noradrenergic
staining, as it is involved in NA synthesis. Inside the neurons, the enzyme TH catalyses the conversion of
tyrosine to L-DOPA. L-DOPA is then decarboxylated by the enzyme DOPA decarboxylase31. Finally, DBH
which is primarily found in noradrenergic neurons, catalyses the hydroxylation of dopamine to form NA31.
Therefore, we opted to perform TH immunohistochemistry in slices containing LC, which we used to
count noradrenergic cell bodies.

the integrity of the nigrostriatal pathways in our animals.
4.3.2 Impact of NA depletion in the nigrostriatal pathway integrity
The loss of dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatal pathway is one of the main pathological
features of PD4. Loss of TH+ cells in the SNpc and decrease of TH+ densitometry in the striatum are
usually observed in most PD models1,4,92. To evaluate the effects of DJ-1 KO mutation and the effects of
DSP-4-induced NA depletion, we performed IHC on the SNpc and the STR.
In previous studies, treatment with DSP-4 seems to affect the dopaminergic system58,64. Lesioning
the LC with DSP-4 leads to a decrease in DA turnover in the striatum and to reduce the extracellular levels
of DA64. Our results showed a significant effect of the experimental group on TH+ densitometry in the
Striatum (F(3,17)=63.402, p<0.001) (figure 12b). Post hoc testing using pairwise comparisons of the
estimated marginal means with Turkey’s correction revealed that T DSP-4 (mean=15.241, SD=1.272)
and DJ-1 DSP-4 (mean=9.414, SD=0.633) had a significantly reduced TH+ fibers densitometry compared
to the WT vehicle group (mean=22.352, SD=1.352, p<0.001). DJ-1 DSP-4 also showed a significant
41

reduction when compared to DJ-1 vehicle group (p<0.005) and compared to WT DSP-4 (p<0.001). The
reduction of ≈42.6 densitometry observed between the T and the D -1 KO vehicle groups may suggest
that the DJ-1 depletion augmented dopaminergic STR degeneration. WT DSP-4 animals showed a
reduction of ≈31.8 fibers densitometry compared to the vehicle group, whereas the D -1 DSP-4 group
showed a reduction of ≈26.7 compared to the D -1 vehicle group and a ≈57.9 reduction compared to
WT vehicle. This result indicates that the DSP-4-induced LC neuron depletion affected the striatal area.
An explanation for this may be the dual role of NA as it has been proposed to exert modulation over
DA39,46,105,106.

protective role of NA on DA neurons. In our analysis, this effect seems to be more significant in the
striatum than SNpc, which could be explained by a retrograde degeneration of dopaminergic neurons
that has been proposed in the literature107. Moreover, the relatively magnitude of the loss of TH fibers in
the striatum evoked by DSP-4 (32% in WT animals and 58% in DJ-1 animals) might partially explain the
mild and non-statistically significant impairments at the motor level, as compensatory mechanisms can
be occurring that hinder a quantifiable behavioral outcome. To address this hypothesis, it would be
important to conduct this experiment in different ages (e.g., 6-, 8- and 12-month-old mice), not only to
take into account the aging factor, but also because it may lead to more pronounced loss of dopaminergic
neurons fibers and consequently, a better-established behavioral phenotype 82,87,90.

5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES
PD is a multifactorial disease and the combination of genetic and environmental factors can
contribute to its earlier development3,9,11. Most of the animal models commonly used to study PD focus
solely on the mimicking of nigrostriatal degeneration. However, it is important to develop models that
allow the study of PD as a multifactorial disease. In our study, we have tried to generate a mouse model
with a genetic background (DJ-1 Knockout) and combine it with a neurotoxin (DSP-4) that ablates
noradrenergic neurons in the locus coeruleus.
Comparing the histological results and the behavioral analysis in this work we were able to explore
the effects of DJ-1 mutation, DSP-4 administration, and the combination of both. Regarding the effect of
the mutation, DJ-1 animals have shown tendencies to impaired motor functioning, especially in gait
evaluated with footprint analysis. In the marble-burying, DJ-1 animals had shown a tendency to develop
anxiety-related to novelty. Histologically, DJ-1 animals showed a reduction of 42.6% in striatal
densitometry and a decrease in the number of TH+ cells in the SNpc compared to WT littermates. Thus,
knocking out DJ-1 in mice seems to lead to a degeneration of the nigrostriatal pathway, leading to motor
and non-motor impairments.
DSP-4 administration had an impact in animal’s balance in the eam alance test and seemed to
be related to the development of anxiety-like behavior. Histologically, DSP-4 evoked a decrease of 83%
and 85% of LC neurons in WT and DJ-1 animals, respectively. In the striatum, DSP-4 administration led
to a decrease of 27% and 32% in fibers densitometry in the striatum in WT and DJ-1 animals, respectively.
However, TH+ cell counts in the SNpc were not different from the respective vehicles. Thus, our results
suggest that the DSP-4-induced LC neuron depletion affected the striatal area but does not seem to affect
the nigral neurons. This suggests that DSP-4 may exert its effect on the nigrostriatal pathway through a
retrograde degeneration which begins in the axons, present in the striatum, and progress to the cell
bodies, located in the substantia nigra107.
The administration of DSP-4 on DJ-1 animals led to behavioral and histological alterations. In the
Beam Balance and MWM test, DJ-1 mice with DSP-4 lesions show impaired balance and swimming
ability. In the contextual fear conditioning, DJ-1 DSP-4 animals showed an improved retrieval of fear
memory. In the Marble Burying and the SST, the administration of DSP-4 seems to develop a tendency
for anxiety and depressive-like behavior. Histologically, in the LC, DSP-4 seemed to have a similar impact
in DJ-1 animals as in WT animals. The administration of DSP-4 in DJ-1 animals led to an exacerbated
degeneration in the striatum (≈58 compared to T vehicle) but not in the SNpc, which can corroborate
44

with the fact that DSP-4 may lead to a retrograde degeneration in the nigrostriatal pathway.
In the future, there are several aspects worth to consider in order to improve this work. Firstly, as
we have been discussing, the lack of statistical differences can be related to the number of animals per
group, or the behavioural tests used. In future studies, it should be considered increasing the number of
animals per group and optimizing the battery of tests chosen.
Secondly, there are parameters to be addressed that can allow to further understand the relation
between the histological degeneration and the lack of behavioral phenotype. In this work we performed
immunohistochemistry analysis that provide information regarding structural alterations. To complement
this, it would be of interest to quantify the concentration of DA, NA and 3-Methoxy-4-Hydroxyphenylglycol
(MHPG), the major metabolite of NA, to assess the effects of DSP-4 on neurotransmitter content, which
would provide a functional analysis of the circuits affected in this model14.
Thirdly, due to time constraints, we were not able to address the impact of LC lesion on other brain
regions involved in non-motor behavioral assessment, such as hippocampus and amygdala, as well as
on the spinal cord. Nevertheless, these analyses would be important to further complement this
histological assessment and have a clearer picture of the impact of noradrenergic degeneration effects
on WT and DJ-1 KO animals. We could also characterize astrocytes and microglia and assess the impact
of noradrenergic degeneration in neuroinflammation, which has been reported to be impaired in DJ-1 KO
mice and DSP-4 treated animals66,70,77,83,108.
Finally, further studies should also include the assessment of other parameters, such as
quantification of genes involved with dopamine-synthesis (by real-time reverse transcription–polymerase
chain reaction) and, to characterize the role of DJ-1 in WT animals, quantify the levels of oxidized and
non-oxidized DJ-1 (through Western blotting)23,74,75,83,88.
Overall, we were able to develop a model able to mimic nigrostriatal and noradrenergic
degeneration described in PD, which led to a mild motor and non-motor behavioral phenotype. This model
resembles the natural progression PD which may provide suitable approach to the study of
neuroprotective therapies targeting different degeneration processes, with an accentuate relevance to
study neuroprotective therapies focused on the LC. Moreover, our work has provided new information
regarding behavioral and histological alterations in both DJ-1 KO and DSP-4 models individually that were
still lacking in the literature.
