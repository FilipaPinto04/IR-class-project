Resumo
Motivando Mentes Médicas: Adaptação da Escala de Motivação
Académica no Âmbito da Teoria da Autodeterminação.
Introdução: A motivação desempenha um papel crucial no sucesso académico e no
desenvolvimento profissional dos estudantes de medicina. Compreender as complexidades da
motivação no contexto da educação médica é essencial para a construção de intervenções eficazes
e sistemas de apoio. Este artigo explora a adaptação da Escala de Motivação Académica (AMS) no
âmbito da Teoria da Autodeterminação (SDT) e da Teoria da Autoeficácia para avaliar a motivação
entre os estudantes de medicina.
Métodos: O estudo utilizou uma metodologia rigorosa para adaptar a AMS ao contexto português,
recorrendo à estrutura da SDT. Duas escalas portuguesas existentes, a MATAMS e a escala de
Ribeiro et al., serviram como as bases fundamentais para o processo de adaptação. A Escala de
Motivação Académica para estudantes de medicina do Minho (Minho-MEDAMS) resultante foi
submetida a procedimentos de validação rigorosos para garantir as suas propriedades
psicométricas e validade.
Resultados: O processo de adaptação resultou numa escala refinada composta por cinco
dimensões de motivação: motivação intrínseca, regulação identificada, regulação externa,
regulação introjetada e amotivação. Análises psicométricas demonstraram uma forte
confiabilidade e validade da Minho-MEDAMS, confirmando a sua adequação para avaliar a
motivação entre os estudantes de medicina no contexto português.
Discussão e Conclusão: A adaptação bem-sucedida da AMS dentro do quadro da SDT
apresenta um instrumento valioso para avaliar a motivação nos estudantes de medicina. A Minho-
MEDAMS oferece uma compreensão abrangente das dinâmicas motivacionais, facilitando
intervenções direcionadas e mecanismos de apoio para melhorar o envolvimento e o sucesso dos
estudantes. A sua validade e confiabilidade tornam-na uma ferramenta prática para educadores,
administradores e investigadores no campo da educação médica. No final, a Minho-MEDAMS
contribui para o avanço de estratégias destinadas a cultivar profissionais de saúde motivados e
proficientes.
Palavras-Chave: Escala de Motivação Académica Motivação, Teoria da Autodeterminação,
Teoria da Auto-eficácia.

Abstract
Motivating Medical Minds: Adapting the Academic Motivation Scale
within the Framework of Self-Determination Theory
Introduction: Motivation plays a crucial role in the academic success and professional
development of medical students. Understanding the intricacies of motivation within the context of
medical education is essential for designing effective interventions and support systems. This paper
explores the adaptation of the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS) within the framework of Self-
Determination Theory (SDT) and of Self-efficacy Theory to assess motivation among medical
students.
Methods: The study utilized a rigorous methodology to adapt the AMS to the Portuguese context,
drawing upon insights from SDT. Two existing Portuguese scales, MATAMS and Ribeiro et al.'s
scale, served as foundational frameworks for the adaptation process. The resulting Minho Medical
Academic Motivation Scale (Minho-MEDAMS) underwent thorough validation procedures to ensure
its psychometric properties and validity.
Results: The adaptation process yielded a refined scale comprising five dimensions of motivation:
intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, external regulation, introjected regulation, and
amotivation. Psychometric analyses demonstrated strong reliability and validity of the Minho-
MEDAMS, affirming its suitability for assessing motivation among medical students in the
Portuguese context.
Discussion and Conclusion: The successful adaptation of the AMS within the SDT framework
presents a valuable instrument for assessing motivation in medical students. The Minho-MEDAMS
offers a comprehensive understanding of motivational dynamics, facilitating targeted interventions
and support mechanisms to enhance student engagement and success. Its validity and reliability
render it a practical tool for educators, administrators, and researchers in the field of medical
education. Ultimately, the Minho-MEDAMS contributes to the advancement of strategies aimed at
cultivating motivated and proficient healthcare professionals.
Key-words: Motivation, Self-determination Theory, Self-efficacy theory, Academic Motivation Scale

Introduction
Motivation serves as the driving force that imbues purpose and direction into behavior,
manifesting itself at both conscious and unconscious levels. The study of motivation encompasses
all aspects of an individual's needs, exploring the intricate processes and structures that link these
needs to behavior. Theories of motivation organize the findings within this realm, with the pursuit
of understanding the "why" of behavior at its core (1-3).
One pivotal theory in the domain of motivation is the Self-Determination Theory (SDT). This
theory offers a profound perspective on human motivation and personality, emphasizing the
significance of internal resources for personality development and behavioral self-regulation (2).
Within the framework of SDT, intrinsic and extrinsic sources of motivation can be defined,
elucidating the roles of intrinsic motivation (IM) and various types of extrinsic motivation (EM) in
cognitive and social development and individual differences. This conceptualization has led to the
creation of a framework illustrating the continuum between motivation types, their regulatory styles,
perceived causes, and relevant regulatory processes as can be seen in Figure 1, removed from
Ryan and Deci, 2000 (4).
Figure 1 – The continuum of Self-Determination Theory removed from Ryan and Deci, 2000, p.72.
Conditions supporting an individual's experience of autonomy, competence, and peer
relationships are considered essential in promoting volitional behaviors and high-quality motivation
and engagement in activities, leading to improved performance, persistence, and creativity.
Moreover, the theory posits that the absence of support for any of these three needs within a social
context can significantly impact an individual's well-being within that context (4).
1

Another theory indirectly contributing to the construct of motivation is the theory of self-
efficacy. This theory underscores the importance of an individual's perception of their personal
capabilities as key determinants of successful outcomes. The theory of self-efficacy aligns with the
democratic ideal that posits all individuals are competent and capable of success given the
opportunities and self-efficacy required to pursue their goals. Embedded within the broader
understanding of the social cognitive theory, the theory of self-efficacy provides a model for
comprehending human emotion, behavior, cognition, and motivation, highlighting the active role
individuals play in shaping their environment (5,6).
These concepts are pivotal in the study of motivation, shedding light on how individuals
modulate their motivational states and how these states translate into outcomes. As emphasized
earlier, the context can significantly impact individuals, making the study of motivation in specific
contexts particularly relevant.
One context associated with high levels of stress, adaptation, resilience, and constant
evolution is the field of Medicine and the extensive journey of medical training. The exploration of
motivation in medical education research has witnessed significant growth, offering intriguing
associations and perspectives that deepen our understanding of medical students' learning
experiences (7-14). Some studies indicate a positive correlation between the motivation of medical
students and the quality of their learning, the learning strategies they employ, their persistence,
and, ultimately, their performance (10,15). Additionally, there is evidence suggesting that while
teachers may not be solely responsible for their students' motivation to learn, their influence is
substantial (16). These findings underscore the relevance and interest of educators in the topic
and the promotion of learning and assessment strategies that enhance the motivation of their
students. In this regard, Viau and Pelaccia have significantly contributed to the field by proposing
a dynamic motivational model associating IM with observed learning outcomes (1). This model is
grounded in three premises: i) the perceived value of an educational activity (or "subjective task
value"), defined as the student's judgment of the interest and usefulness of completing a proposed
activity based on their goals (16,17); ii) perceived self-efficacy (or "perceived competence"),
defined as the student's judgment of their ability to successfully engage with the proposed
educational activity (16); and iii) perceived controllability, defined as the degree of control a student
believes they have over the progress of an activity (16).
To comprehensively understand and study motivation in medical students, the availability
of suitable and contextually adapted scales is imperative. The Academic Motivation Scale (AMS), a
2

widely used 28-item scale with seven subscales assessing three types of IM, three types of EM and
amotivation (AMOT), has been utilized in many studies to evaluate motivation in medical students
(18-21). However, considering the distinct characteristics of medical courses and the need for high
levels of motivation there arises a rationale for the development of a motivation scale tailored for
medical students.
Therefore, this project proposes the development of a motivation scale, designed to be
simple and intuitive for medical students, with the aim of measuring their motivation levels related
to learning acquisition. The scale seeks to assess each dimension of motivation outlined in the
SDT. This approach enables the quantification of each dimension of motivation, offering insights
into the distribution across the motivation’ spectrum. With this data, it becomes possible to tailor
incentives to the internal needs of medical students, understand how different incentives affect
different students, and identify medical students who are intrinsically motivated or demotivated for
targeted interventions.
3

Objectives
The main objective of this study was:
- To develop a valid and reliable motivation assessment scale tailored for medical students
based on the SDT and through the adaptation of the AMS.
4

Materials and Methods
Study Design
We conducted an exploratory mixed-methods study to adapt the AMS based on a protocol
for contextualized measurement scale adaptation (22). To do this, we took two different
perspectives of the validation process: a qualitative and a quantitative phase, creating different but
very valuable information.
This study took place at the School of Medicine of the University of Minho (EM-UM). The
experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Minho (CEICVS-
121/2023) (see Appendix 1). Adherence to the Helsinki Declaration and the Convention on Human
Rights of the Council of Europe was strictly observed (23,24). Written informed consent was
obtained from each participant before data collection.
A comprehensive literature review on motivation scales, with a primary focus on the AMS,
including its variations, design, validation process, and impact assessment, was conducted through
various databases (7-21).
Qualitative Phase
For the qualitative perspective, two distinct approaches were employed to address the
research questions: Steps 1 and 2. In both steps, interviews were conducted following a semi-
structured guideline.
In Step 1, we used the grounded theory approach (25), which serves as a method for
exploring the foundational aspects of a construct (26). This step involved investigating participants'
comprehension of the concepts, constructs, and the new context. Grounded theory ensures a
specific understanding of a concept in the new context, emerging organically from the collected
data. Interviews continued until no additional information was acquired during the process.
In Step 2, we employed think-aloud methods (27) to test the understanding of the
measurement scale in the new context. Think-aloud reasoning is a method where participants
verbalize their thoughts when choosing an answer for the items and express their reasoning and
interpretation of the choice made.
In both interviews, no answer options were presented, as the focus was solely on
respondents' reasoning.
5

Participants
We selected several groups of individuals involved in the issue of motivation in medical
students, including medical students of different curricular years, alumni, and faculty members.
These groups were defined during the interview process and the qualitative interviews for step 1,
using the grounded theory approach, were conducted with 13 participants, comprising 6 medical
students, 1 alumnus, and 6 faculty members. For step 2, we had a total of 10 medical students,
2 alumni and 3 faculty members.
Measures
Following best practices, we used not only the original AMS, but also two already validated
translations into Portuguese of the AMS, as the original framework (18,28,29).
Academic Motivation Scale
The AMS stands as a cornerstone in assessing students' drive for learning, widely
employed across educational studies. The scale was originally developed by Vallerand et al. (18)
in French and, in 1992, translated to English, creating the AMS. The scale comprises 28 items
rated on a seven-point Likert scale, that are divided in seven subscales that respect the different
dimensions of motivation described in the SDT: IM, EM and AMOT. Interestingly, Ryan and Deci,
established with the SDT, the dimension of IM as a global construct, while Vallerand et al. divided
this dimension into three subtypes: IM - to know; IM - to accomplish; and IM - to stimulate. The
first subtype, “To Know”, refers to when a student does something for the pleasure and satisfaction
experienced while learning, the second subtype “To Accomplish”, refers to when a student does
something for the pleasure and satisfaction experienced while trying to accomplish things, and the
third type “To Stimulate”, refers to when a student does something in order to experience
stimulating sensations (30). Another interesting choice for the authors of the AMS was the exclusion
of EM – Integrated Regulation (EMIR) from the original SDT motivation spectrum, explaining that
this dimension of motivation only appears later in adult life with much less expression in the
academic range (31). With these concepts present, the AMS was created with 28 items, distributed
over the 3 dimensions defined by the SDT: 4 items for AMOT, 12 items for EM and 12 items for
IM. The AMS follows some basic principles based on the SDT that pose that stronger positive
correlations should be present between adjacent dimensions, opposed to the dimensions that are
6

further apart in the self-determination spectrum. Also, the strongest negative correlation should
appear between AMOT items and the IM items (18,30,32,33).
It is important to highlight that several studies have demonstrated a robust internal and
external reliability across diverse research studies and countries (30,31,34-38).
Analyses
For Steps 1 and 2, qualitative content analysis was employed. Initially, the first author read
four interview transcripts, inductively coding phrases, and clauses as thematic elements to create
a coding system. This coding system was then discussed and validated with another author.
Subsequently, it was used to code four additional interviews and, if necessary, complemented with
additional codes. Finally, the coded interviews and all remaining interviews were analyzed using
the more elaborate and validated coding system.
Scale Development and Application
Building upon the insights gained from the qualitative phases of Steps 1 and 2, a new
adapted scale was crafted – the Minho Medical Academic Motivation Scale (Minho-MEDAMS). The
qualitative findings provided a rich understanding of participants' comprehension of motivation
concepts and their perspectives in the specific context under investigation, which, in turn, resulted
in the adapted scale presented. The first version of Minho-MEDAMS was designed in the same
model of the original AMS, with the influence of both the Portuguese scales, composed by 28 items
and conceptually divided in 7 subdimensions: IM to Know, IM to Accomplish, IM to Stimulate,
EMIR, EM – Identification (EMID), EM - External Regulation (EMER) and AMOT. The scale was then
presented to the participants through a WordPress website developed by the researchers.
When designing the Minho-MEDAMS, the authors also proposed to present the scale as a
visual analogue scale (VAS). The VAS, first described by Hayes and Paterson, are rating scales in
a continuous graphical format in which the participants are shown a straight line where they can
mark any point on this line, corresponding to their degree of agreement (39). One of the advantages
of using a VAS, is that answers are not restricted to a certain number of response options, but very
fine gradations can be measured. They avoid the systematic bias of values resulting from scale
coarseness, a problem present when using items with distinct response categories that are meant
to measure a continuous latent variable (40,41).
7

After the adaptation of the scale, it was also discussed the best way for delivering the
Minho-MEDAMS to the targeted population. With this in mind, an unexpected, yet valuable, skill
development journey unfolded. Recognizing the increasing significance of an online presence in
the academic and professional spheres, the author embarked on a self-directed learning path to
master website development, particularly utilizing the WordPress platform. The decision to delve
into website development was driven by the realization that an effective online presence can
significantly amplify the impact of academic research. As the digital landscape becomes
increasingly integral to scholarly communication, we identified the need to acquire proficiency in
building and managing a website to showcase the research endeavors and create a platform for
knowledge collection and dissemination. WordPress, renowned for its user-friendly interface and
robust capabilities, emerged as the platform of choice. The decision to learn WordPress was not
only driven by its widespread adoption but also by its flexibility in customization and its ability to
cater to both novices and seasoned developers.
The Minho-MEDAMS, with visual analogue items and shaped by the qualitative
groundwork, was then applied to participants in the quantitative phase were the results not only
provided valuable insights into the overall robustness of the adapted scale but also shed light on
the specific contributions of each scale item to the measurement of motivation in the unique context
of medical education at the EM-UM.
This seamless progression from qualitative exploration to the development and quantitative
assessment of the adapted scale underscores the rigor and comprehensiveness of our study
design, ensuring that the resulting instrument is finely tuned to capture the intricacies of motivation
within the targeted academic setting.
Quantitative Phase
In this phase we assessed the psychometric properties and performance of the Minho-
MEDAMS, adhering to criteria for scale construction (42). The effectiveness of the quantitative
measurement instrument in the new context was evaluated through several methods.
Participants
To assess the validity of the scale, we included all medical students from the first to the
sixth year of medical school at the EM-UM. The sample size was calculated using a ratio of 10:1
8

(10 respondents for each scale item), with a minimum of 280 respondents for a total of 28 items
(42). The final sample size for the scale validation was 281 respondents.
Analysis
To test the normality assumption, we used the following rules-of-thumb: absolute skewness
(Sk) and kurtosis (K) values lower than 3.0 and 8.0, respectively, indicate normal distribution. Items
with Sk and K over the limit were eliminated. In order to assess the validity and reliability of the
Medical Students Motivation Scale, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), Confirmatory Factor Analysis
(CFA) and internal reliability Cronbach’s alpha scores were performed (43).
Internal consistency
Internal consistency reflects the extent to which the scale items are intercorrelated, or
whether they are consistent in measuring the same construct. In this work we used the coefficient
alpha (Cronbach’s alpha) to estimate the internal consistency of the scale. Cronbach's alpha ranges
from 0 to 1 with α- 0 indicating no internal consistency, and α- 1 reflecting perfect internal
consistency. Cronbach's alpha of at least 0.70 was considered to indicate adequate internal
consistency.
Factor Analysis
To assess the construct validity of the Medical Students Motivation Scale, an EFA was
performed. To test the suitability of the scale for the factor analysis, we used: 1) Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin
(KMO), which measures sampling adequacy, ranging from 0 to 1, in which higher values mean
higher suitability and a value of 0.6 is a suggested minimum and 2) Bartlett’s test of Sphericity,
which tests the hypothesis that the correlation matrix is an identity matrix, which would indicate
that the variables are unrelated and therefore unsuitable for structure detection. Taken together,
these tests provide a minimum standard which should be passed before a factor analysis is
conducted. After the suitability tests, we used the Maximum Likelihood analysis as the extraction
method. On a second moment, a CFA was performed on the remaining variables, to test how well
the measured variables represent the number of constructs.
CFA tests a hypothesized structure with model fit statistics and parameter estimates (44).
Model fit indices need to be judged against recommended thresholds. In this analysis we used the
Comparative Index of Fitness (CFI) with values greater than 0.90 being considered to be a good fit.
9

Finally, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) was examined. Values below 0.06
indicate a good fit, and values that are above 0.08 indicate reasonable approximation errors.
10

Results
Qualitative Phase
Step 1: Is the Motivation Concept relevant and understood in a new context?
For Step 1, the author used a previously determined coding system, obeying to seven
parameters related to the SDT and the AMS motivation’ organization: IM - to know; IM – to
accomplish; IM - to stimulate; EMID; EMIR; EMER; AMOT. It was applied to the interviews with 13
participants, comprising 5 medical students, 2 alumni, and 6 faculty members.
The participants gave several perspectives regarding motivation in medical students, which
allowed the creation of a concept map regarding the topic. All participants perceived that motivation
is a major player for medical students’ success, giving different justifications for this perception.
“Motivation allows you to overcome those most difficult moments” and it is also “the
stimulus that makes people have the willing, and the volition to do and achieve their goals.”
On the other hand, some participants talked about how an amotivated student can be
problematic, saying “I think that the student who is not motivated is not going to be the student
that the society needs.”
Regarding IM, the participants where asked what factors might play a key role in
intrinsically motivating medical students and these factors appeared in several ways. Some referred
to the feeling of accomplishment that is in fact present for medical students.
“The social factor, the community factor of wanting to help, of wanting to evolve into
society, of wanting to help people” and that drive to “always want to be better and always want to
know a lot, which is also a direct or almost direct link between the more they study and the more
they know, the better they will be able to help from a cognitive and technical point of view”.
Other intrinsic dimensions were also noticed, such as the need of control of the situation
“The person feels that they have a role in that process, that they have some control over what is
asked of them” which may contribute to the notion that they are “building a path you're proud of.”
Looking into IM to know, participants also talked about the excitement of learning and
surpassing their own obstacles and difficulties. They count this as an important part of feeling
intrinsically motivated because it gives them the drive to continuously pursuing better results –
“I’m learning something new and I’m starting to make new connections – it’s starting to get
11

interesting which is very stimulating.” They also highlighted that “studying medicine is challenging
but there is great satisfaction in learning things I’d never learned before.”
When asking about EM, the answers of the participants were more fluid – “It is easier to
think about what I want to obtain in return of my effort, than to think about what might drive me to
start the effort from the beginning.”
Regarding EM for identification, in which an external stimulus is perceived by the self as
an individual choice or even when the external stimuli is the self, this specific type of motivation is
one of the most frequently referred during the interviews – “I believe that being a doctor is a noble
profession and I want that for myself.” On the other hand, some said that while studying medicine,
they seem to have difficulty stopping review the content for exams because they do not want to fail
themselves and would be embarrassed if they failed.
When accounting for the society effect, some behaviors are indeed driven by external
stimuli that the student interiorizes as a socially accepted behavior. Some of the participants said,
“a lot of medical students chose to study medicine because when they finish high school, they
have good grades, and everyone expects them to choose medicine.” While others added that “a
good medical student will always study because that what they’re supposed to do.”
Reaching the last dimension of EM, the true external regulation, many participants focused
on external stimuli that directly affect medical students’ motivation. One of the biggest factors was,
undoubtedly the assessments they are put through. External validation in form of a grade is
perceived as being both a positive and negative stimulus for studying medicine. One participant
referred that “most medical students are highly driven and competitive people, they compare
themselves by their grades, but the system makes them that way.” The system referring to the
several selections they go through in which they are selected by grade, mostly overlooking the
remaining curricula. Other external stimuli are high salaries, social prestige, the possibility of having
access to “good quality of life”.
Finally, AMOT was regarded by most as a major problem when seen amongst medical
students and perceived as being related to mental health issues such as depression, generalized
anxiety and burn-out.
Step 2: Are the motivation scale and its items understood?
In Step 2, we tested whether the items of the newly adapted scale were understood in the
new context: medical education. Based on the results of Step 1, we developed adapted items to
12

measure motivation in medical students. In this step, the participants were given the opportunity
to read the full adapted scale and were asked to explain in their own words the purpose of the
scale. Most participants referred that the scale’s purpose is to assess motivation in medical
students while adjusting for specific stimuli that particularly apply to medical students. Some
participants had some difficulties regarding the main question of the scale: “Why do I go to medical
school?”. When asked why, most participants said that it seems more adjusted to specify “Why do
I study medicine?” to be focused on the activity throughout the medical degree, while the first
question seemed more appropriate for students starting medical school.
When responding to the items by thinking aloud, participants perceived that the questions
were specific for medical students and focused on specific stimuli that refer to different dimension
of motivation. Initially items were written in the third person such as “Because they feel pleasure
and satisfaction when learning new content about Medicine” but participants raised concerns if
medical students would truly relate to the question, making the items more easily understood if
they were written in the first person. Adjustments were made throughout the scale’s items to
rephrase the items.

As the first step of the scale’s analysis, we tested the scale’s items for its normality
assumption, using absolute skewness (Sk) and kurtosis (K) values. One item with absolute
skewness scores above 3 and kurtosis higher than 8 showed a deviation from the normal
distribution; for that reason, item P19 was excluded from the scale.
In line with the literature, in this study EFA and CFA were simultaneously performed, in
order to guarantee that all items of the new scale were grouped adequately by double-check
analysis. If the EFA results indicate that each item is appropriately grouped and is corroborated by
CFA results, showing a fit model, then it is safe to conclude that the items in the cluster can
precisely measure the intended construct.

To confirm the EFA, we performed a CFA on a 141 random sample of the results and on
a first model compiling five latent variables, as suggested by the EFA, revealing inadequate
goodness of fit. The next step for the authors was to assess the R-squared value of each individual
item. The R-squared of items refers to the proportion of variance in each observed item that is
explained by its underlying latent construct or factor, measuring how well the latent factor accounts
for the variability observed in each item. A higher R-squared value indicates that the latent factor
explains a larger portion of the variance in the observed item, suggesting a stronger relationship
between the item and the underlying construct, being interpreted as evidence of good construct
validity, indicating that the item is a reliable and valid indicator of the intended construct. The post-
hoc analysis of the model revealed low R-squared values for items P1, P10, P13, P18, P20, P22
and P27. When looking closely to these items, the authors concluded they were distributed between
the different factors, so, if removed, the authors considered there weren’t significant changes to
the overall construct. For these reasons, those items were excluded from the final scale.

As such, the final model has good statistical results (χ2=199,249, p=<0.001, df=125,
χ2/df=1,594, RMSEA=0.065, SRMR=0.079, GFI = 0.869, and CFI = 0.935). Also, the
relationship between the dimensions of IM and AMOT showed a negatively correlation and, in the
same way, the sub-dimensions of EMID and AMOT showed a negative relationship between them.
The sub-dimensions of EMIR and EMER did not show a negative correlation with AMOT, but the
strength of the correlation was low. There was positive relationship between the dimension of IM
and the sub-dimensions of EM, with special remark to the EMID sub-dimension showing a very

Discussion
In this work we focused on the assessment of motivation levels in medical students in the
Portuguese context. To do this, we followed a conceptual structure focused on the SDT and the
theory of self-efficacy, both pivotal in the study of motivation, shedding light on how individuals
modulate their motivational states and how these states translate into outcomes. One of the most
important scales in this area that follows the SDT is the AMS. As previously showed, the AMS is a
widely used scale to measure motivation in the academic setting and has been validated in various
languages and different settings. However, we believe that the medical context deserves an
adjusted perspective to fully understand what drives medical students’ motivation. For this reason,
in this Thesis we adapted the AMS to the medical students’ context and submitted the new Minho-
MEDAMS to a structured and rigorous validation.
The process of adaptation and validation was performed in two different phases, starting
with a qualitative analysis of motivation in the context of a Portuguese medical school, including
not only medical students but also faculty and alumni, and moving on to a quantitative analysis
focused on the validation of a new adapted motivation scale for medical students – Minho-
MEDAMS. On the first phase we understood that motivation for medical students follows the
purposes of the SDT and we identified several aspects that should be reinforced on a motivation
scale for this population, such as the community’s acceptance, the choice of specialty and the
notion of acquiring a good quality of life. Through several interviews, we were able to formulate
items that respected the dimensions of motivation identified in the original scale but were tailored
for medical students. The items were then submitted to syntax and comprehensive analysis by
medical students, faculty and alumni to ensure they were clear and easily understood. Through
this process, a first sample of the Minho-MEDAMS was created.
The scale was then made public by the use of a website specifically created for the
purpose: motivationmeded.com. Importantly, the website serves as an efficient hub to collect data,
but also as a dynamic hub for disseminating our findings, engaging with a wider audience, and
fostering collaborations within the academic community.
After the results were collected through the website, the authors did a complete analysis
to assess the validity and reliability of the Minho-MEDAMS. On the first step, one item (P19) was
removed from the scale, to ensure a normal distribution of scores, using kurtosis and skewness
cutoff values. We then proceeded to the Factor Analysis using the EFA and CFA. The EFA results
19

were satisfactory, with a KMO value of 0.862, and the extraction of 5 factors and the exclusion of
P4 and P25. On the CFA, we analyzed two descriptive indices: the CFI and the RMSEA. On the first
model assessed, the CFA revealed inadequate goodness of fit. In order to improve the model and
create a more efficient scale, the authors identified the items with lower R-squared values: P1, P10,
P13, P18, P20, P22 and P27; these items were distributed along the different identified factors
but also the different conceptual subtypes defined at the beginning of the process: IM, EMID and
EMER. At this point, the Minho-MEDAMS’s final version consisted of 18 items, 6 items for IM, 9
items for EM (3 items for EMID, 3 items for EMIR and 3 items for EMER) and 3 items for AMOT.
On note, the authors carefully analyzed the reduction of the scale’s items, with concern of
maintaining the theoretical structure of the SDT while addressing the redundancy of some of the
items within each dimension of the scale. We believe that the final version of the Minho-MEDAMS
follows the principles of the SDT with significant representation of each dimension of motivation
and the spectrum itself. This way, the structural theory that was used as the conceptual framework
of this work was respected and applies to the Minho-MEDAMS. Regarding internal consistency,
measured by Cronbach’s alpha, Minho-MEDAMS reveals high consistency for all five dimensions
and excellent results if considered the total items of the scale which reflects that Minho-MEDAMS
is an appropriate instrument to be used to measure motivation amongst medical students.
As previously elucidated, the Minho-MEDAMS was conceived through the lens of SDT and
serves as an adaptation of the AMS (18). To develop this scale in Portuguese, the authors drew
upon two existing Portuguese scales that had already translated and validated the AMS into this
language (28,29). One of these scales, the MATAMS, focused solely on mathematics and extracted
seven factors, aligning closely with the dimensions of motivation in the AMS (29). Conversely, the
scale by Ribeiro et al. that directly translated the AMS to the Portuguese language, was validated
with only 5 factors that corresponded to the following dimensions of motivation: IM, AMOT, EMIR,
EMER and EM – Integrated Regulation (28). This is an interesting finding that goes in line with the
results of this work, where only 5 factors were extracted with the difference lying in the exclusion
of the EMID dimension and inclusion of the EM subdimension of Integrated Regulation in the work
of Ribeiro et al., whereas the Minho-MEDAMS included the EMID and excluded Integrated
Regulation. These two dimensions lie closely on the SDT spectrum and that might be a sufficient
explanation of why these two subdimensions might even be presented as one given their proximity
in the spectrum (45). Another interesting topic of the Minho-MEDAMS is the lack of differentiation
between the IM subdimensions. This follows the SDT that addresses IM as a whole and does not
20

describe the subdimensions proposed on the AMS (4,18). For this reason, we believe that the
Minho-MEDAMS respects the SDT continuum and is a conceptually valid choice to remain only with
the broader dimension of IM.
With these results, we propose that the implementation of the Minho-MEDAMS will help
medical educators get a better insight into the motivations of medical students, thereby
empowering medical schools to tailor interventions and adapt strategies to bolster motivation levels
while addressing the individualized needs of each student. In the end, motivation could contribute
to elucidate the discrepancies in academic performance among students who undergo similar
instruction. Understanding the diverse motivational orientations of students might be able to equip
faculty and medical schools with the insights needed to implement more targeted and precise
interventions aimed at fostering academic success across student populations.
There are some limitations that should be considered when interpreting these results: i)
the design of the study did not allow convergent and divergent validation mechanisms, which could
be important to establish construct validity; ii) the number of participants, despite being adequate
for validation, could still be improved in order to strengthen the results of this work; iii) students
were recruited from a single Medical School; and iv) students from the 1st year of medical school
had a slightly bigger representation in this study which may have influenced some of the results.
Looking ahead, the application of the Minho-MEDAMS can another step closer to obtain
valuable insights into the evolving landscape of medical student motivation, enabling educators
and institutions to tailor interventions that align to the specific needs and aspirations of their
learners. Moreover, the longitudinal use of the scale can facilitate tracking and understanding of
motivational shifts throughout the medical education journey, thereby informing curriculum
development and support services to optimize student engagement and success. In the future, the
scale might be adaptable to other contexts within medicine but outside the academia, with potential
applications in residency training and continuing medical education.
21

Conclusion
In summary, the development and validation of the Minho-MEDAMS marks a new
development in the area of medical education and motivation assessment, in the Portuguese
context. By effectively capturing five essential dimensions of motivation with good psychometric
properties, this scale presents itself as a valuable tool for examining and comprehending the
motivational dynamics among medical students. The comprehensive validation process
underscores the reliability and validity of the Minho-MEDAMS, confirming its suitability for use
within the Portuguese context. Consequently, medical schools and faculty can employ this scale to
evaluate and address the motivational needs of medical students, thereby fostering a supportive
learning environment conducive to academic success and professional growth.
