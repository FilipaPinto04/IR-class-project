Desenvolvimento e Implementação de Atividades Profissionais Confiáveis na Formação
Específica de Anestesiologia em Portugal – estudo piloto
Resumo
Introdução: Nas últimas décadas, a educação médica pós-graduada está em mudança para um novo
paradigma baseado em competências e centrado no resultado. As atividades profissionais confiáveis
(APC) são “unidades de trabalho” que são observadas e que podem ser avaliadas, estando em linha com
a mudança de paradigma educacional. A formação específica em Anestesiologia, em Portugal, ainda não
contempla um currículo baseado na competência, sendo ainda residual a avaliação no local de trabalho.
Este estudo piloto pretende promover o desenvolvimento e a implementação das APC no programa da
formação específica em Anestesiologia em Portugal. O presente estudo pretendeu (1) caracterizar o
interesse da comunidade de anestesistas em APC; (2) promover ações de formação; (3) elaborar uma
APC piloto; (4) implementar e avaliar a APC piloto. Métodos: (1) Através de um inquérito à comunidade
de anestesiologistas foi realizada a caracterização demográfica, interesse e familiaridade com as APC;
foi questionado o interesse em melhorar os conhecimentos, bem como a motivação para fazer parte do
processo de implementação. (2) Foram realizadas reuniões de formação e criada uma plataforma online
para e-learning. (3) Foi desenvolvida uma APC piloto em grupo focal e (4) a sua implementação e
avaliação foi realizada com recurso a uma aplicação móvel. Resultados: (1) 142 participantes
responderam ao inquérito inicial. Dos respondentes, 53% eram internos; 47% eram especialistas, dos
quais 42% possuem mais de 10 anos de experiência clínica. Verificou-se que 61% não conheciam o
conceito de APC e 98% desejavam aumentar o seu conhecimento. Mais, 93% consideram relevante iniciar
a implementação de APC e 92% gostariam de fazer parte do processo de implementação. (2)
Promoveram-se 6 reuniões de formação, realizadas em 5 hospitais, envolvendo um total de 167
especialistas e 51 internos. (3) A APC elaborada foi “Indução anestésica em doente ASA I/II com jejum
pré-operatório, sem sinais de via aérea difícil, para procedimento de baixo risco”. (4) A APC piloto foi
implementada em 5 hospitais. A avaliação formativa desta APC vai ser realizada através da aplicação
móvel preparedEPA. Conclusão: A implementação de APC na formação específica em Anestesiologia é
exequível. Internos e especialistas devem ser parte integrante da implementação. O primeiro passo deve
ser a formação dos formadores.
Palavras-chave: atividades profissionais confiáveis, anestesia, avaliação no local de trabalho

Development and Implementation of Entrustable Professional Activities in Portuguese
Anesthesia Residency program – a pilot study
Abstract
Introduction: In the last decades postgraduate medical education is changing to a competence-based
system centred on outcome. The Entrustable Professional Activities (EPA) are a “units of work” that can
be done, observed, and assessed and are aligned with the new educational paradigm. Portuguese
anesthesia residency training does not include a competence-based curriculum, and in this domain has
residual workplace-based assessment. This pilot study wants to promote the development and the
implementation of EPA in Portuguese anesthesia residency programs. The present study aimed to (1)
characterize Portuguese stakeholder’s interest on EPA; (2) promote faculty development on Competence-
Based Medical Education (CBME) concept; (3) develop one pilot EPA and (4) implement and assess the
pilot EPA in a small group of Portuguese hospitals. Methods: (1) A survey was distributed to the
Portuguese Anesthesia Community. Demographic characterization; interest and familiarity with EPAs;
sources of knowledge; interest in improving knowledge on the field as well as motivation to be enrolled in
implementation process, were surveyed. (2) Faculty development initiatives were created as presential
meetings and online e-learning sessions. (3) A pilot EPA was developed after focus group discussions. (4)
The pilot EPA was implemented through workplace-based assessment, using a mobile application.
Questionnaires to evaluate the experience and the usability of the application will be sent and analyzed.
Results: (1) 142 participants answered the initial survey. Among the participants, 53% were residents and
42% were consultants with 10-20 years of experience. 61% of participants were not familiar with the
concept of EPA; 98% wanted to improve their knowledge on EPA; 93% found it relevant to initiate EPA
implementation, and 92% wished to be enrolled in the process. (2) 6 faculty development initiatives
meetings were conducted in 5 hospitals. A total of 167 consultants and 51 residents were invited. (3)
Induction of Anesthesia for ASA I/II fasting patient with no difficult airway for a low-risk procedure, was
the pilot EPA developed. (4) The pilot EPA was implemented in 5 hospitals, and its assessment was done
using mobile the preparedEPA application. Conclusions: The implementation of EPA in Portuguese
anesthesia residency programs is feasible. Residents and consultants should be recruited for the
implementation process. The first step should focus on faculty development.
Keywords: entrustable professional activities, anesthesia, workplace-based, assessment

1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. Competence-based Medical Education
1.1.1. Historic Perspective and Definition
A significant evolution has been undertaken in medical education guidelines over the last decades, shaped
by changes in society, advances in technology and healthcare and the theoretical support of effective
learning methods. Before the 20th century, medical education policies were heterogeneous and erratic. At
that time, no practical courses were mandatory, and it was possible to graduate without having contact
with patients. In 1910, Flexner reported key problems in medical education, namely lack of
standardization and integration and failure to focus on professional identity formation (Riggs, 2010). His
report conducted the first reform in medical curricula towards a science-based model where patient care,
investigation and teaching were connected (Irby, 2010). In the middle of the century, another educational
reform was implemented, the problem-based learning model. In this approach, the training experience
was based on clinical rotations and relied on exposure to specific contents for a fixed and limited time,
regardless the individual acquisition of competencies. In the late 80s, with the foundation of the
Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME), undergraduate and postgraduate medical
training become regimented, which reduced training heterogenicity between American medical schools.
Two contributions were fundamental for the educational paradigm change. The first was the pyramid
framework to guide assessment in health professionals conceptualized by Dr. George E. Miller. He
organized a model in a four-step pyramid representing from the base to the top, the domain of knowledge
(knows), competence (knows how), performance (shows) and act (does). The last step represents the
outcome of medical education defined by World Health Organization (WHO) – a professional acting in the
real workplace with proficiency in accordance with the local conditions and community needs (Custers,
2018). Miller also highlighted the need to embrace subjective clinical impressions on the domain of the
does from the workplace, and to develop strategies for a reliable assessment on performance in real
world (Miller, 1990).
The second contribution was given by Kolb that introduced the experimental learning as a framework that
describes adults learning throughout experience (Reynolds, 2020). The permanent cycle of concrete
experience, reflection on the experience, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation allowed
the learner to be self-conscious about their performance and needs of improvement and simultaneously
foster the acquisition of competences necessary to act as a professional. This process motivates self-
2

regulation learning, which is fundamental for the life-long learning and the creation of the professionalism
identity (Brydges, 2015).
Two institutions started the foundations of the CBME in Canada and United States of America. The
CanMEDs (Canadian Medical Education Directives for Specialists) framework for CBME and Outcome
Project from ACGME (Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education) clarified and provided
structure to the pathway for the change in medical education with relevance on education and
assessment, in this new model (Ten Cate, 2017). Both institutions defined domains of competence
(CanMEDS – Medical expert, Communicator, Collaborator, Leader, Health advocate, Scholar and
Professional and ACGME – Patient care and procedure skills, Medical Knowledge, Practice-based learning
and improvement, Interprofessional and communication skills, Professionalism, System-based practice)
that guided and supported the theoretical foundations of the CBME. Those competences should and can
be taught, observed, and assessed in the clinical setting. So, the previous model of medical education,
based on time and theoretical acquisition was replaced by an educational framework centered on the
outcome and focused on the learner pathway for the acquisition of competence in the real workplace,
regardless of time (Flenk, 2010).
In conclusion, competence-based medical curriculum is a structure for education that emphasizes
learner’s and patients’ outcomes, learner’s abilities, and competences, promotes learner’s engagement
in the learning process and is time-flexible (Frank, 2010).
1.1.2. Milestones
Following the creation of the CBME curriculum, in 2013, with competencies and sub-competencies, the
ACGME formally introduced milestones. A milestone represents a significant point in the development of
a professional who transitions from an observer to an independent practitioner (Englander, 2017). These
milestones serve as a formative and summative assessment process for learners, supervisors and
institutions, guiding residents’ self-assessment and reflection. These, subsequently, lead to the creation
of learning plans to achieve the recommended goals (ACGME, 2020). Given that the focus of CBME is
centered in patient and learner, the assessment of the achieved milestones is only feasible within the
clinical work. The emphasis on clinical practice ensures a holistic evaluation aligned with the goals and
principles of CBME.
3

1.1.3. Workplace-based assessment
In a CBME model, the workplace serves as the primary context for most of the learning. It is within this
setting that the integration of knowledge, skills and attitudes take place (Ten Cate and Billet, 2014). Over
the years, many tools, such as mini-CEX and multiple observable practice activities (OPA) have been
developed and implemented for workplace-based assessment in many medical schools and postgraduate
programs (Norcini, 2003; Kelleher, 2020). The intended outcome utilizing these instruments is to
enhance the quality of clinical practice and ultimately, ensure patient safety.
Considering that, from a CBME perspective, the “outcome is a physician that can practice medicine at a
defined level of proficiency, in accord with local conditions, to meet local needs”, there is a requirement
for the development of workplace-based assessment to support proficiency in the clinical setting (Ten
Cate, 2017). Entrustable Professional Activities (EPA) have emerged as the link connecting competency
frameworks, milestones and workplace-based assessment in clinical practice (Ten Cate, 2017; Tanaka,
2023).
1.1.3.1. Entrustable Professional Activity
In 2005, Ten Cate defined Entrustable Professional Activity (EPA) as a specific task within a specialty that
professionals should perform without direct supervision, once sufficient competence has been
demonstrated (Ten Cate, 2005). Those “units of work” include knowledge, skills and behavior and may
reflect one or more competencies across various domains of competence acquired during the training.
As tasks in the clinical work, they are observed, entrusted, and subject to assessment by supervisors
daily, in a formative or summative approach. The adoption of EPAs allowed residency programs to provide
support to learners throughout their training while ensuring competence gains across all domains before
complete autonomy. Trainees are assessed on EPAs based on a certain level of entrustment from their
supervisors. During their training program, residents progress from a high level of supervision, functioning
as direct observers to competent experts capable of performing clinical work independently and even
supervising other residents or junior doctors. Therefore, the concept of EPA aggregates both final
objectives of medical schools and residency programs: (1) confirming knowledge, competence,
performance, and the ability to independently provide patient care – the tetrad of Miller’s concept and (2)
ensuring and protecting the quality of patient care – the confidence/trust on health professional’s care
(Ten Cate, 2021).
4

1.2. Competence-based Medical Education in Residency Programs
It is important to highlight that many residency programs still rely on structural and process-based
assessments. In these programs, residents are exposed to contents during a standard period, and the
assessment is limited to the acquired knowledge. They operate on a mentorship model, with a national
examination serving as a certification for independent practice. Consequently, in many countries there
remains a gap between the CBME approach in medical schools and the assessment methods employed
during residency programs. Establishing a continuum from medical school to postgraduate programs is
imperative.
The implementation of CBME, milestones and EPAs in medical specialties postgraduation program is
growing worldwide. Many postgraduate programs have adopted competency frameworks and delineate
the domains of competence and milestones each trainee need to achieve during the training. Specifically
for postgraduate anesthesia training program, this approach has been adopted, developed, and
implemented in many countries namely in Canada (Bailey, 2024), the Netherlands (Wisman-Zwarter,
2016), Switzerland (Tanaka, 2023) and USA (Porter, 2022; Ten Cate, 2010). The outcomes from these
programs serve as promising models for other countries to be followed (Savoldelli, 2016; Ten Cate,
2021).
1.2.1. Portuguese Anesthesia Residency Program
The anesthesia residency program in Portugal is well-organized and structured under the guidelines set
forth by the Anesthesiology College from Portuguese Medical Association. The most recent reform,
implemented in 2016, defined a training period of 60 months (Diário República, 2016). This training
includes 54 months of clinical rotations in a time-dependent sequence, followed by an additional 6 months
for optional practice defined by the trainee. General and specific goals for all the clinical rotations are
established, along with well-structured competencies expected to be acquired during the training. The
assessment of residents is primarily retrospective, occurring at multiple time-points: (1) at the end of each
clinical rotation; (2) at the end of each year; and at (3) the end of the entire training period throughout a
national examination for certification (Portaria nº92-A/2016). In the beginning of the training program,
each resident is assigned a supervisor who monitors their professional development. However, as the
training progresses, residents work with different tutors based on the specific clinical rotation they are
attending. Despite the tradition of supervisor responsibility for providing feedback on trainee’s
5

performance and outcomes, opportunities for formative feedback and reflection can be scarce or even
absent, during the training. This creates a gap between the CBME approach of the training program and
assessment based on daily practice. To bridge this gap, integrating programmatic assessment focused
on learner progression and outcomes in Portuguese anesthesia training program is crucial for the next
years. The introduction of EPAs could be considered as the initial step in this process (Heeneman, 2021;
Torre, 2021).
1.3. Keys steps for EPA Implementation
The transformative change to a competence-based medical curriculum in the context of a residency
program requires key elements to guarantee success. In a recently published work, Stoffman enumerated
three elements that contribute to the achievement of this process: (1) Stakeholders’ engagement and
leadership; (2) Faculty development; and (3) Educational and technological support (Stoffman, 2022).
Each element will be discussed separately.
1.3.1. Stakeholders’ engagement and leadership
Intrinsic collaboration and the creation of a community of practices for the implementation of CBME are
strategies for success, as they facilitate interaction, motivation, and improvement among various
elements (Railer, 2020). Indeed, stakeholders’ engagement, ranging from educational leaders to patients,
is essential for the success of an innovative curriculum reform (Boet, 2016).
1.3.2. Faculty development
Faculty development is fundamental for the implementation of CBME. These activities are considered as
planned interventions designed to improve individual knowledge, skills, and behaviors in educational field,
and is one of the challenges in the context of CBME (Dath, 2010). Faculty development equips supervisors
and mentors with the competence to provide adequate active learning strategies and reliable assessment
tools in a programmatic approach. The traditional teacher that evaluated only knowledge, in the CBME
context is a facilitator that can track his learner progression and provide constructive feedback, daily
(Fraser, 2016). Simultaneously, faculty members are conscientious about the principles of CBME and
contribute to disseminating the concept within their departments. Their role is essential for a consistent
and reliable assessment. The development of these skills in faculty members also enhances
6

trustworthiness and commitment within the individuals, the organization, and the system, thereby
contributing to a successful implementation. Many strategies have been studied to promote faculty
development such as workshops, presentations, ground rounds, email updates, video tutorials and online
modules (Steinert, 2006). However, a continuum approach to faculty development is needed to maintain
high levels of supervisors’ performance. Residents also need profound understanding of the CBME
curriculum to actively engage with the concept. They also play a role on faculty development as their
feedback can contribute to the improvement of supervisors.
1.3.3. Educational and technological support
Although dependent on the local capabilities, technological support can promote and enhance the
engagement of residents and supervisors with the assessment (Mann 2020). The increased number of
assessments in a CBME curriculum may create a burden on supervisors who are already overloaded with
clinical, administrative, and educational tasks. These requirements pose obstacles for the ongoing
process of CBME and can potentially compromise its implementation.
1.4. Development of Entrustable Professional Activities
Each residency program has autonomy to decide the number and nature of competencies, milestones
and EPAs that best represent its specialty. This program must be designed in accordance with the
institutions and community’s needs in which it is integrated. In the process of developing an EPA, there
are two main issues to consider: (1) mapping of competencies and sub-competencies of the specialty
and (2) identifying the real professional work/task that must done, representing and characterizing the
specialty (Ten Cate et al., 2015; Fernandes et al., 2022). In recent years, residency programs have
chosen to develop their EPAs, initiating their implementation and assessment in a daily practice. The
methodologies used to define EPAs in different medical fields have been diverse. Nevertheless, there is a
need for introspection into what characterizes the daily work performed by these qualified doctors.
Numerous strategies have been described in literature to validate EPAs among experts including expert
meetings discussions (Cheng, 2016; Stalmeijer, 2014), surveys (Boyce, 2011), delphi procedure
(Fessler, 2014) nominal groups technique (Touchie, 2014), and interviews. Regardless of the approach
used for validation, an EPA must be relevant for the clinical work and be aligned with the recommendation
for graduates in the program (Ten Cate et al., 2015).
7

1.4.1. Technology for assessment
There is a growing development of technology for medical education. The implementation of EPAs in a
graduated program demands multiple assessments on each working day, with multiple sources of
formative and summative data that are challenging to store in a paper-based system. The use of off-the-
shelf electronic platforms for assessment, available in mobile phones and tablets with easy accessibility,
favors and supports the opportunities for evaluation. In recent years, a new web and mobile application,
named preparedEPA, was developed and implemented in many Residency Programs over Europe and US.
This application enables the documentation of workplace-based assessment towards programmatic
assessment (Marty, 2022).

2. AIMS
This work was conducted to promote the development and the implementation of Entustable Professional
Activities (EPA) in the Anesthesia Residency Program in Portugal.
The study aims to:
(1) Characterize the interest and knowledge regarding EPAs among consultants and residents in
Portuguese Anesthesiology Departments.
(2) Promote faculty development for stakeholders (consultants, supervisors, mentors, tutors, and
residents on Competence-Based Medical Education (CBME) and EPAs.
(3) Develop a pilot EPA based on a focus group discussion.
(4) Implement and assess the developed EPA in the Portuguese Anesthesiology Residency Program,
supported by a mobile application

3. METHODS
3.1. Ethical approval
This study was conducted following relevant guidelines and regulations and received institutional approval
from the Ethical Committee for Institute of Life and Health Sciences (CEICVS006/2024) of School of
Medicine, University of Braga, Braga, Portugal (Appendix 1). Participants in the study included consultants
and residents from Anesthesia Departments from Portuguese Hospitals. Participation was voluntary and
all participants gave verbal and written informed consent.
3.2. Study date
The study took place at the School of Medicine, University of Minho, Braga, Portugal, from October 2022
to February 2024.
3.3. Study Design
The study was developed in 4 steps.
3.3.1. First step – Online survey
An online survey was developed by the author, using a Google form. The questionnaire was unrestricted
to members (consultants and residents) of the InAnesthesia Community (through WhatsApp channels), a
group consisting of trainers and trainees from all secondary and tertiary hospitals with anesthesia
residency programs in Portugal, totaling 532 participants. The survey remained open for 60 days after
the initial message, a single follow-up reminder was sent to all members, 30 days after the initial invitation.
Written consent was necessary for participation.
The study aimed to characterize responders in terms of gender; professional category
(resident/consultant); year of residency program/years working as consultant; hospital where they
performed clinical activity. It also investigated participants’ familiarity with the use of EPAs in anesthesia
training program and their sources of knowledge on the concept of EPAs. Participants expressed their
interest in being involved in the EPA implementation process and provided their opinion on the relevance
of starting the implementation of EPAs in Portuguese anesthesia training program, each according to a
5-point Likert scale (Strongly disagree, Disagree, Neutral, Agree, Strongly agree) (Appendix 2). From a
12

population of 532, an estimated sample of 224 responders (50%) is required for a confidence level of
95% and a confidence interval (CI) of 5%.
3.3.2. Second step – Faculty development
Well-planned faculty development initiatives were offered by the principal investigator to the Portuguese
Anesthesia Departments interested in participating in the study, in accordance with the answers from the
previous survey. Conceptual presentations on CBME and EPAs were conducted at official department
meetings, either in person or online. Simultaneously, an unrestricted online e-learning platform was
developed to support the theoretical foundations of the CBME and EPA available on demand. The platform
included videos, relevant articles and presentations and was available to the entire anesthesia community
at: https://padlet.com/inanestesia/anesthesia-faculty-development-in-assessment-jjczmm9b2il6nqiw
(Appendix 3). This platform was facilitated by a shared password provided by the principal investigator.
3.3.3. Third Step – Development of an Entrustable Professional Activity
Educational coordinators of the anesthesia departments were invited via email to participate in two online
exploratory research sessions through a focus group discussion. The aim was to gather opinions for the
elaboration of one EPA for use as a pilot. These sessions were conducted between November 2022 and
March 2023. The moderator prepared a presentation for the first meeting on the foundations of CBME
and the role of EPA in the workplace-based assessment and formulated a set of semi-structured questions
for discussion. After the first meeting, experts individually sent their list of possible EPAs by email to the
moderator. One week before the second focus group, the moderator sent back a compiled list of EPAs.
The focus group deeply analyzed the list, identified the EPA, and developed its description, competences,
and sub-competences. (Figure 1)

3.3.4. Fourth step – EPA Implementation and Assessment through preparedEPA
This study used the preparedEPA as a technological tool to facilitate the implementation, constructive
feedback and formative assessment of the pilot EPA. Specific settings were created in the platform for
this study (PortugalAnesthesia-prepared), namely name of Portuguese hospitals; details of the pilot EPA,
namely title, description, competencies and sub-competencies, and assessment methods. All relevant
information was in Portuguese language. Portuguese hospitals with Anesthesia training programs were
recruited to initiate the implementation of the pilot EPA. In each hospital a face-to-face meeting was
conducted to explain the implementation process to directors, consultants, and residents. A manual
explaining the pilot EPA was created and sent by email to all participants. (Appendix 4) An user’s manual
was also developed to facilitate the use of the mobile application. (Appendix 5) The implementation of the

DISCUSSION

5. DISCUSSION
The results from the present work show that despite a significant lack of knowledge there is a strong
willingness for the implementation of EPA in anesthesia training, as most responders showed interest in
participating in the process.
The implementation of the EPAs in a residency training program represents a significant change in the
paradigm of the current training and assessment model. In Portugal, the change is particularly demanding
since all training programs are centrally regulated by Specialty Colleges of the Portuguese Medical
Association. So, any adjustment on educational model needs to be well-organized, structured, and based
on deep research and must have reliable results. This system ensures an informed decision-making
process based on elevated standards of quality but makes the process time-consuming.
Pilot studies on medical education have been useful to (1) demonstrate the feasibility of processes; (2)
find weaknesses and gaps in knowledge, process implementation and validation and (3) obtain the
primary data for the intended outcomes (Hassan, 2006). The results from this work highlight many gaps
that need to be addressed before the implementation of EPA. Familiarity with the concept of CBME and
EPAs was relatively low for most of the responders (61%), especially if we look for the group of consultants
(45%). This means that many concepts of CBME, milestones, workplace-based assessment and EPAs are
missing. This fact is not surprising, as many departments and institutions worldwide faced similar
difficulties in the beginning of the implementation process (Tannenbaum, 2019).
Faculty development on medical education is, thus, in this context, a critical step in the process of
curriculum implementation (Leslie, 2013; Sirianni, 2020). Improving knowledge, skills and attitudes
among faculty members increases confidence and competence in educational clinicians who lead the
implementation strategy (Steinert, 2011). Nevertheless, faculty development is time-consuming for
mentors and attendants. Anesthesia departments organize weekly meetings for clinical presentations,
institutional and organization needs that are attended by most of the consultants and residents. In this
study, the principal investigator presented the department meeting to promote faculty development in
competence-based medical education, in general, and in EPAs, in particular. Due to the anesthetists’
work schedule, it was not always possible for the entire clinical staff to attend the meetings. Lack of time
among anesthetists is a commonly identified barrier to accomplish faculty development initiatives (Fraser,
2016). So, to increase those opportunities, the author created extensive online documentation on a digital
platform – Padlet, accessed through a link. In this online platform, the most relevant articles, videos,
links, and presentations were easily accessible during the study period. Padlet has been considered a
23

helpful platform for learning since it allows the organization of contents on different boards and is user-
friendly (Naamati-Schneider, 2023).
The next step was to validate an EPA. Focus group discussions have been used as a technique to validate
EPAs (Ten Cate, 2015; Cheng, 2016). In the present study, only consultants in anesthesia were recruited
for the group discussion, missing the constructivist paradigm of the method (Stalmeijer, 2014). In fact,
other relevant stakeholders such as members of the Anesthesia Council form Portuguese Medical
Association, department directors and residents from different years of training could be enrolled on the
group. While this was not the ideal composition of the focus groups it was, nevertheless, the starting point
of discussion on this field in Portugal. The pilot EPA is aligned with the requirements from the blueprint
and publications from the Anesthesia College from Portuguese Medical Association as the competence
of a resident at the end of the first 24 months of training (Diário da República, 2016).
The preparedEPA is user-friendly, and because it is based on mobile phones and tables, allows workplace
application and immediate feedback (Woodworth et al, 2021). Although there are many relevant biases
related with the use of the application, namely resident, supervisor or patient factors, validity and interrater
reliability that need to be explored, we expect that supervisors and residents become proficient with the
frequent use of preparedEPA since we believe that it can support the implementation of EPA in Portuguese
Anesthesia Residency Program.
Despite the significant amount of work done, this study represents a small drop in a big ocean. This pilot
study demonstrated that it is feasible to start the implementation of Entrustable Professional Activities in
the Portuguese Anesthesia Training Program. But there is a lot of work that must be done, mainly in the
domains of leadership and faculty development; competence-based curriculum developers and on
assessment tools.
Limitations and Future Perspectives
The study has some limitations that need to be highlighted. First, the response rate to the initial survey
was relatively low; however, it must be said that the results are in accordance with the average of online
survey response rate in medical literature (18-20%) (Tait, 2015).
The commitment to faculty development was relatively low, considering the entire anesthesia community.
One way to improve participation is to consider mandatory enrollment for supervisors or to provide
incentives for faculty development attendance (Steinert, 2016). Although the present work only explores
24

FD in the domain of theoretical foundations of CBME, there are other initiatives that are equally relevant
and complementary, namely observations of workplace-based assessment, feedback and coaching on
assessment, self-evaluation and reflective, and consideration to build a community of practice (Favreau,
2017; Baldwin, 2017; Bray, 2021).
Although there were qualitative appreciations from the use of the padlet platform by faculty members, it
was not possible to count the number of accesses which could have provided estimates of the impact of
the training sessions on the audience and of the interest raised by it.
Meanwhile, the present work was already presented to the president of Anesthesia College from
Portuguese Medical Association and there are lot of perspectives for this curricular change. In addition,
the project is ongoing with programed FD initiatives in other three hospitals and the next step will be to
present on the annual meeting of Portuguese Society of Anesthesiology and to all the Portuguese
Anesthesia Community.
Finally, the major limitation of the study relates with time. Medical education takes time and resilience,
with many initial steps forwards and backwards. However, the seeds are sown, and the springtime is
coming. I hope I can contribute to this innovative curriculum transformation.

CONCLUSIONS

6. CONCLUSIONS
This study demonstrated that there is an interest, among the Portuguese Anesthesia community, in
changing to a competence-based medical education curriculum in Portuguese Anesthesia Residency
program with the focus on workplace-based assessment. The result from the present study suggests that
despite widespread general awareness of the CBME concept and its major constructs, there is a gap in
knowledge related to Entrustable Professional Activities. Therefore, there is a need for faculty development
towards the implementation of the CBME and EPA that should be generalized at a national level.
PreparedEPA can be a useful application to facilitate the transition to EPA and workplace-based assessment.

Background and Aim
The last 30 years were marked by a profound change in medical education from a time-based to an
outcome-based system. This change allows a healthcare professional to follow their learning path focused
on the acquisition of competence in different aspects of patient care, regardless of time. But to ensure
that the clinician acquired the needed clinical competence in patient care, new strategies of workplace-
based assessment are needed.
Entrustable Professional Activities are units of professional work, that must be done during a clinical day,
that can be assigned, measured, and observed. It is a way to teach and assess. At the end of the training
program, a supervisor can trust a learner to do the EPA independently or unsupervised. Simultaneously,
EPA are a way to track learners’ path and identify those who need more support. in the acquisition of
competences
The concept of EPA contains knowledge, skills and behaviour and is better suited for assessment in
everyday work, since it is a more holistic view of a concrete observable clinical activity. One EPA contains
many competences and sub-competences. The table 1, presents the EPA to be used. The metric for
competence is the level of “required supervision”, in a 5 points scale. (table 2) Because ever clinical
situation is a suitable opportunity for learning and assessing, the use of a friendly application could
improve the engagement of residents and tutors in the process.

Título

Descrição
Esta Atividade profissional confiável inclui o período desde a entrada do doente na sala de
indução/sala operatória até ao início da cirurgia. Esta EPA inclui a avaliação de conhecimento,
competências técnicas e comportamento.
Competências e Sub-competências
- Preparou-se de acordo com o plano anestésico escolhido (conhece o doente e ambiente de trabalho)
- Cumpre o regulamento de higiene local
- Assegura condições de segurança e de utilização de dispositivos necessários para a intervenção
anestésica (ventilador, monitor, sistema de perfusão, etc)
- Garante a realização das check-list de segurança (Cirurgia Segura da OMS, lista de verificação pré-
indução)
- Comunica adequadamente com o paciente (explica o que vai acontecer)
47

- É capaz de colocar acesso venoso periférico ou central (se necessário)
- É capaz de colocar cateter arterial (se necessário)
- É capaz de manusear uma via aérea não difícil
- Realiza a monitorização adequada ao doente e à intervenção
- Inclui a análise de dados laboratoriais do paciente, sendo capaz de interpretar os seus resultados e
de atuar em conformidade (reconhecendo problemas e complicações e tratando-os)
- Administra os medicamentos necessários de forma adequada, segura e atempada (incluindo
antibioterapia – se necessário)
- Garante a profundidade adequada da anestesia e o relaxamento muscular (se necessário) para o
início da intervenção cirúrgica
- Organiza o transporte do paciente da área de indução para a sala operatória de forma segura (se
aplicável)
- Realiza os registos clínicos de forma correta
- Comunica de forma eficaz com os restantes membros da equipa do bloco operatório (equipa de
enfermagem, auxiliar, equipa cirúrgica, especialista, colega de internato)
- Delega tarefas de forma adequada (se necessário)
- Re-avalia a situação continuamente e ajusta as prioridades de forma dinâmica (tem um plano B)
- Pede ajuda de forma adequada (conhece os limites da sua experiência)
Métodos de avaliação
Observação direta; entrustment-based discussion; case-based discussion

A preparedEPA é uma plataforma online que permite o feedback contínuo pelos supervisores, tutores e orientadores durante

da avaliação baseada nas atividades profissionais confiáveis (EPA-entrustable professional activities).

difícil.

APPENDIX 10 – Developed pilot EPA
83

Atividade Profissional Confiável desenvolvida
pelo projeto
Sara Hora Gomes
Título
Indução de anestesia em doente ASA 1 ou ASA 2, em jejum, sem via aérea difícil previsível para intervenção de baixo
risco.
Descrição
Esta Atividade profissional confiável inclui o período desde a entrada do doente na sala de indução/sala operatória até
ao início da cirurgia. Esta EPA inclui a avaliação de conhecimento, competências técnicas e comportamento.
Competências e Sub-competências
- Preparou-se de acordo com o plano anestésico escolhido (conhece o doente e ambiente de trabalho)
- Cumpre o regulamento de higiene local
- Assegura condições de segurança e de utilização de dispositivos necessários para a intervenção anestésica (ventilador,
monitor, sistema de perfusão, etc)
- Garante a realização das check-list de segurança (Cirurgia Segura da OMS, lista de verificação pré-indução)
- Comunica adequadamente com o paciente (explica o que vai acontecer)
- É capaz de colocar acesso venoso periférico ou central (se necessário)
- É capaz de colocar cateter arterial (se necessário)
- É capaz de manusear uma via aérea não difícil
- Realiza a monitorização adequada ao doente e à intervenção
- Inclui a análise de dados laboratoriais do paciente, sendo capaz de interpretar os seus resultados e de atuar em
conformidade (reconhecendo problemas e complicações e tratando-os)
- Administra os medicamentos necessários de forma adequada, segura e atempada (incluindo antibioterapia – se
necessário)
- Garante a profundidade adequada da anestesia e o relaxamento muscular (se necessário) para o início da intervenção
cirúrgica
- Organiza o transporte do paciente da área de indução para a sala operatória de forma segura (se aplicável)
- Realiza os registos clínicos de forma correta
- Comunica de forma eficaz com os restantes membros da equipa do bloco operatório (equipa de enfermagem, auxiliar,
equipa cirúrgica, especialista, colega de internato)
- Delega tarefas de forma adequada (se necessário)
- Re-avalia a situação continuamente e ajusta as prioridades de forma dinâmica (tem um plano B)
- Pede ajuda de forma adequada (conhece os limites da sua experiência)
Métodos de avaliação
Observação direta; entrustment-based discussion; case-based discussion
