SUMMARY
Medical education is a discipline in constant evolution, where advancements in medical knowledge,
technology, education and societal trends converge. It adapts to a highly mutable landscape and is
permeable to emerging trends and innovations. Among the most transformative developments in recent
years is the widespread proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI), that is revolutionizing all sectors of
society, including healthcare and education. In this thesis, AI is succinctly defined, on its evolution and
key characteristics.
Central to this dissertation is the exploration of the intersection between AI and medical education. A
review of literature reveals the myriad applications of AI-associated technologies across different
domains of medical education. From automated assessment and feedback mechanisms to
personalized learning platforms, virtual patient simulations, and intelligent tutoring systems, AI-driven
tools offer unparalleled opportunities for enhancing learning outcomes and optimizing educational
processes. Evidence suggests that the integration of AI-based strategies produces tangible benefits,
facilitating the generation of teaching materials, delivering effective feedback, and enhancing
performance at both educational and operational levels. However, despite the clear advantages
demonstrated by these technologies, their widespread adoption in medical school remains limited.
More, AI is not a topic in most programs. This gap leaves students and healthcare professionals with a
unilateral understanding of AI, overlooking its potential to revolutionize healthcare delivery and medical
education. Addressing this disparity is paramount, as it ensures that future generations of healthcare
providers are equipped with the requisite skills and knowledge to navigate a rapidly evolving healthcare
landscape.
Keywords: Artificial Intelligence; Assessment and Feedback; Curriculum Design; Medical education;
Personalized Learning.

RESUMO
A educação médica é uma disciplina em constante evolução, onde convergem avanços no
conhecimento médico, tecnologia e educação. É por definição altamente permeável a tendências e
inovações emergentes. Entre os desenvolvimentos mais transformadores dos últimos anos está a
proliferação da inteligência artificial (IA). Esta afeta todos os setores da sociedade, incluindo saúde e
educação. Nesta tese, a IA é definida sucintamente, a sua evolução e características-chave.
A interseção entre IA e educação médica é central nesta dissertação. É apresentada uma revisão da
literatura sobre as inúmeras aplicações das tecnologias associadas à IA em diferentes domínios da
educação médica nomeadamente nos mecanismos de avaliação e feedback, plataformas de
aprendizagem personalizada, simulações de pacientes virtuais e sistemas de tutoria inteligente. A
evidência sugere que a integração de estratégias baseadas em IA produz benefícios tangíveis,
facilitando a geração de materiais de ensino, fornecendo feedback eficaz e melhorando o desempenho
em níveis educacionais e operacionais. No entanto, apesar das claras vantagens demonstradas por
essas tecnologias, sua adoção generalizada nas escolas médicas permanece limitada. Além disso, a IA
não é um tópico na maioria dos programas.
Essa lacuna deixa estudantes e profissionais de saúde com uma compreensão unilateral da IA,
limitando o seu potencial. Abordar essa disparidade é fundamental para garantir que as futuras
gerações de alunos e profissionais de saúde estejam equipadas com o conhecimento necessário para
navegar em cenários de assistência médica em rápida evolução.

1.1. A brief history of artificial intelligence
The ability to use intelligence to conceptualize and construct models about intelligence itself is the
fascinating outcome of billions of years of evolution and has prompted the creative minds of
philosophers, scientists and novelists throughout the times. What some authors refers as meta-
intelligence comprises the understanding, control, and coordination of higher cognitive processes
(Sternberg, 2022; Sternberg et al., 2021). In other words, it involves the ability to teach and utilize
intelligence – and artificial intelligence (AI) – effectively. For centuries various cultures have had myths,
stories, and legends featuring artificial beings endowed with intelligence or consciousness. The myth of
Pygmalion, in which the sculptor Pygmalion creates a statue that comes to life or the story of Talos, a
giant automaton made of bronze that protected the island of Crete in Greek mythology, are just
amongst the many tales that survived through our times (Mayor, 2018). However, only much later,
always at the borders between philosophy, biology and technology, theories and models to explain how
the mind works start to arise. From the XVII century on several philosophers contributed to the
development of symbolic logic and formal systems of reasoning thereby providing the theoretical
framework for later advancements in mathematics, logic, and computer science. Particularly, René
Descartes (1596-1650), Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) and George Boole (1815-1864) are
considered that most contributed to the foundations. Descartes is best known for his philosophical
work, including the formulation of Cartesian dualism and his famous statement "Cogito, ergo sum".
While he did not directly contribute to symbolic logic in the same way as Leibniz and Boole, his
emphasis on rational thought and clear, systematic reasoning laid the foundation for later developments
in logic. Leibniz made significant contributions to mathematics particularly he developed calculus,
2

philosophy, and logic. He also developed the binary number system, which represents numbers using
only two digits (0 and 1) and is fundamental to modern computing and forms the basis of digital
computers. Finally, Boole was also a mathematician and logician, best known for his work on Boolean
algebra, a branch of mathematics that deals with logical operations and binary relationships that laid
the foundation for symbolic logic and modern formal logic. Boole's book "The Laws of Thought" (1854)
introduced the concept of Boolean algebra. His algebraic notation for logical operations, such as AND,
OR, and NOT, became fundamental to the development of digital logic circuits and computer science.
The idea of mechanical manipulation of symbols is considered one of the most influential concept
in the history of AI (Abrahams, 1969). This concept refers to the idea that human thought processes
can be represented and understood as the manipulation of abstract symbols according to predefined
rules or algorithms. This notion is central to symbolic AI, also known as classical AI or Good Old-
Fashioned Artificial Intelligence (GOFAI; Haugeland, 1985). In the context of AI, it involves creating
computer programs that operate on symbolic representations of knowledge, rules and reasoning
processes. These programs use logical rules and algorithms to manipulate symbols, perform
computations and make decisions. Alternative approaches, like machine learning (ML; see below)
emerged attempting to overcome the limitations of symbolic AI namely difficulties with tasks that
involve handling uncertainty, learning from large amounts of data or dealing with complex, unstructured
information.
It was from this interplay between philosophical inquiry into the nature of thought and
mathematics that eventually led to the development of early AI systems, particularly in the mid-20th
century when advances in computing technology made it possible to implement symbolic reasoning
processes on computers (see Table 1).

1.2. Machine learning
Among the significant milestones in the history of machine learning (ML), these particular events
warrant special recognition. Alan Turing (1912-1954) was the first to propose the idea of a “learning
machine” in his seminal paper “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (Turing, 1950). Although his
focus was more on artificial intelligence in general and the question of whether machines could exhibit
intelligent behavior comparable to humans, his ideas laid the groundwork for later developments in ML
– see also (Copeland, 2004). However, the actual term “machine learning” was coined by Arthur
Samuel (1901-1990); he developed the first self-learning program, a checkers-playing program, which
improved performance with experience (Samuel, 1959). The development of the percepton (Rosenblatt,
1957), one of the earliest models of an artificial neural network, contributed to the development of
neural network-based learning algorithms (Sarker, 2021). Finally, Tom M. Mitchell (1951- ) provided a
4

formal definition of ML in his influential book “Machine Learning” (Mitchell, 1997). There he states
“The field of machine learning is concerned with the question of how to construct computer programs
that automatically improve with experience.” Or, on a more formal wat “A computer program is said to
learn from experience E with respect to some class of tasks T and performance measure P, if its
performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E.”. This formal construct still is
transversal to the ML field despite its enormous growth with contributions from researchers in computer
science, statistics, mathematics, and other disciplines. It encompasses a wide range of algorithms and
techniques that enable computers to learn from data and make predictions or decisions without being
explicitly programmed.
1.3. The widespread use of Artificial Intelligence
In an almost imperceptibly way AI is becoming part of almost all our activities (see Table 2) –
critical assays (Bryson, 2018). The impact is immense. Just to mention a few, AI enables the
automation of repetitive tasks, leading to increased efficiency and productivity. Algorithms can process
large amounts of data and identify accurate and reliably patterns invisible to the human eye. Vast
amounts of data can be analyzed to provide personalized experiences and recommendations. Also, AI
algorithms can forecast future trends and behaviors based on historical data, allowing to allocate
resource and mitigate risks as well as to enhance safety and security by detecting and preventing
potential threats in areas such as cybersecurity, surveillance, and autonomous vehicles. AI-powered
technologies can increase the inclusion of individuals with disabilities by providing assistive features
such as speech recognition, text-to-speech, and computer vision applications. It goes without saying
that on the other side many have argued on the perils of such technologies. Among those more on the
public eye are: i. job displacement due to automation; ii. bias generated by the data used to train AI
which might led to unfair or discriminatory outcomes; iii. privacy concerns and misuse of sensitive
information associated with the AI capability to use large amounts of personal data (Qian et al., 2024);
and iv. environmental impact due to energy consumption (and/or carbon emissions) associated with
the large-scale computing resources. All these aspects raise ethical dilemmas associated with
algorithmic accountability, autonomy and potential misuse in social control and autonomous decisions.
Regarding this latter aspect, it important to note that some AI algorithms, particularly deep learning
models, operate as "black boxes" making it challenging to understand how they arrive at their decisions
or predictions. Overreliance on technology might be catastrophic in virtually all areas of human activity

2. Methods
Following the objectives presented above, a comprehensive scope review on the intersection of artificial
intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in medical education, was performed. The search strategy
combined the terms "artificial intelligence" and "machine learning" with the Medical Subject Headings
(MeSH) term "Medical Education". The search was executed on PubMed in January 2024. Relevant
articles were identified through the assessment of titles and abstracts.. The resulting information was
organized into distinct topics pertinent to the application of AI and ML in medical education. These
topics include Personalized Learning, Virtual Patient Simulations, Intelligent Tutoring Systems,
Automated Assessment and Feedback, Predictive Analytics, and Curriculum Design and Optimization.
Each topic was scrutinized to extract key insights, methodologies, and findings from the selected
literature. Data extraction involved categorizing studies based on their objectives, methodologies,
participants, outcomes, and implications for medical education. The synthesis of information from
diverse sources facilitated a comprehensive understanding of the current landscape of AI and ML
applications in medical education. A critical evaluation of this literature is presented in the final chapter
of the thesis.

3.1. Medicine throughout times
Medicine has undergone dramatic transformations propelled by scientific advancements, cultural
shifts, and the efforts of a community of physicians and researchers – (Bynum, 2008); see for a
possible timeline (Hajar, 2015). Initially, mythology, religion and superstition were part of the practice.
The rudiments of what can be considered a systematic records and practice can be traced be to
ancient civilizations in Egypt, Mesopotamia, China, and India. The ancient Greeks, particularly figures
like Hippocrates, contributed significantly to the foundations of Western medicine and is frequently
considered the pioneer of modern medical practice for his advocacy of meticulous observation,
documentation and ethical conduct (Finger, 1994). The Renaissance a resurgence of scientific
exploration takes place. Through meticulous dissections anatomists like Andreas Vesalius (1514–1564)
challenged old anatomical misconceptions, laying the groundwork for modern anatomical
understanding (Finger, 1994). But it was in the 19th and 20th centuries that an unparalleled
advancement in medicine started until today driven by breakthroughs in microbiology, physiology, and
pharmacology and supported by unprecedent technological advances culminating in diagnostic
techniques like X-rays or MRI just to mention 2 examples. In the 21st century, the pace of medical
evolution shows no signs of slowing, with emerging technologies like AI, nanotechnology and
regenerative medicine poised to revolutionize healthcare delivery and outcomes, all underpinned by the
enduring commitment of healthcare professionals.
11

3.2. Medical education
Medical education evolved in parallel with medicine and its practise, reflecting its complexification
and concomitant need of specialization – a number of reviews and books have been published (Custers
and ten Cate, 2018; Fulton, 1953). It is difficult to establish an origin but many scholars consider that it
began in ancient Greece with the formation of “schools” such as that of Cos which Hippocrates was
part of (Finger, 1994). Medical education as a formal discipline began to take shape in the 18th and
19th centuries with the establishment of medical schools and the standardization of medical training
programs. Prior to this period, medical education was often informal and apprenticeship-based, with
aspiring physicians learning through apprenticeships with practicing doctors or through self-directed
study of medical texts. The Flexner Report, published in 1910 by Abraham Flexner, also played a
significant role in shaping modern medical education (Flexner, 1919). The report evaluated medical
schools in the United States and Canada, advocating for higher standards in medical education,
including rigorous admission criteria, standardized curricula, and emphasis on scientific principles
(Drake, 2014). As a result of the Flexner Report, many medical schools underwent reforms to meet
these new standards, leading to the establishment of modern medical education as a formalized
discipline. Presently, medical education reflects in the one hand the complexification of medical
practice and, on the other, the multitude of pedagogical instruments that surfaced in the past decades.
It is a multidisciplinary field that encompasses a wide range of topics and disciplines, including
curriculum development, teaching and learning methods, assessment and evaluation, faculty
development, and educational research.
3.2.1. Curriculum Development
Curriculum development involves the creation, evaluation, and revision of educational curricula
to ensure alignment with educational guideline, evolving needs of healthcare delivery and meet
accreditation standards – see for a recent article selection on the topic (Lai et al., 2024). It
encompasses topics such as instructional design, competency-based education, and integration of
basic sciences and clinical medicine. While core to the educative process, frequently those involved in
the process have no specific training (Schlegel et al., 2021; Thomas and Kern, 2004).
3.2.2. Teaching and Learning Methods
Medical education research explores various pedagogical approaches and teaching techniques
to enhance student learning and engagement. This includes active learning methodologies such as
12

problem-based learning, team-based learning, simulation-based education as well as the use of
technology in medical education. AI systems can be used to generate teaching materials. It has been
suggested the use of generative adversarial networks (GANs) to create novel data, upon training on
existing data sets. For instances, in the context of radiology GANs could be used to produce synthetic
images for training without data protection and confidentiality concerns (Arora, 2020).
3.2.3. Assessment and Evaluation
Assessment research examines methods for measuring learner competence, performance, and
progression throughout medical training. This includes the development of reliable and valid
assessment tools, such as standardized exams, objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs), and
workplace-based assessments.
3.2.4. Faculty Development
Faculty development focuses on supporting the professional development of medical educators,
including faculty members and clinical preceptors. Research in faculty development addresses topics
such as teaching skills training, mentorship, career advancement, and promoting a culture of teaching
excellence within academic medical institutions.
3.2.5. Student Selection and Admissions
Studies in this area explore admissions processes, selection criteria, and predictors of success
in medical school. Research may examine the impact of admissions policies on student diversity,
academic performance and career outcomes.
3.2.6. Professionalism and Ethics
Medical education emphasizes the importance of instilling professionalism, ethical reasoning,
and communication skills in future healthcare providers. Research in this area explores strategies for
teaching and assessing professionalism, as well as interventions to address lapses in professional
behavior.
3.2.7. Interprofessional Education
With the recognition of the importance of collaborative practice in healthcare, there is growing
interest in Interprofessional Education (IPE). Studies in IPE investigate approaches to fostering
13

teamwork, communication, and mutual respect among healthcare professionals from different
disciplines.
3.2.8. Continuing Medical Education
Research in Continuing Medical Education (CME) focuses on strategies for lifelong learning and
professional development among practicing clinicians. This includes evaluating the effectiveness of
CME activities, identifying barriers to participation, and exploring innovative delivery formats.
3.3. Artificial Intelligence in Medical Education
History shows that medical education as a discipline has not only been open to innovation but it
also thinks prospectively (Sherbino et al., 2014). In this regard, the incorporation of AI and associated
technologies in the different medical education areas is not surprising. Such is reflected in the number
of publications dedicated to AI in medical education that is steadily increasing since 2017 (see Figure
1). With the exception of reviews and commentaries – see for instance (Garg, 2020; Masters, 2019;
Wartman and Combs, 2018; Webster, 2021) – the vast majority of the published studies can be divided
in 2 main groups. The first includes studies that focus on the inclusion of AI in medical education
programmes; in other words, AI education in medicine. The studies on the second group focus on the
application of AI and AI-based technologies on the different fields of activity of medical education as
defined above (2.2.1. – 2.2.8.) and in the resulting outcomes of such implementation.

implementation. Surveys conducted among undergraduate students across 17 Canadian medical

positively. However, these very students also expressed agreement that opportunities for AI-related
learning were either non-existent or inadequate (Pucchio et al., 2022). Similar sentiments, particularly
regarding the scarcity of opportunities, were echoed in studies conducted in Turkey (Civaner et al.,

AI-driven decision support systems (AI-DSS), were evaluated in a positive way particularly regarding

of narrative feedback, facilitating rapid organization and analysis (Booth et al., 2023). Still on feedback,

performance (even if marginally) when compared to the control situation (Shah et al., 2023). Beyond

and decision-making abilities among medical trainees (Merritt et al., 2022). The feasibility of AI-driven
communication simulations for pediatric residents was explored and the outcomes were suggestive of a
positive impact in fostering effective patient interactions.
Regarding assessment, the use of ChatGPT-4 as an adjunctive tool in creating a comprehensive
multiple-choice examination was demonstrated. This highlights its potential to aid in educational
assessment (Klang et al., 2023). The evaluation of ChatGPT's performance in obstetrics/gynecology
problem assessment demonstrates its ability to provide accurate responses even in complex clinical
scenarios, showcasing its potential in clinical decision-making support (Riedel et al., 2023). In both
cases, i tis demonstrated that ChatGPT can be used for instances for on-demand informal assessment.
Moreover, AI technologies such as gamification tools with recommendation systems, resulted in
improved learning outcomes (Castellano et al., 2024). However, in one case the comparison between
AI-based assessment (AIBA) and expert-based assessment (EBA) uncovered potential weaknesses in
the AIBA approach, including issues related to construct underrepresentation (Johnsson et al., 2024).
Finally, 1 study assessed the validity of AI-generated scoring measures in laparoscopic simulation
videos, revealing global similarities between video reviews conducted by AI and humans (Ryder et al.,
2024). Such showcases the potential of AI to bring the assessment processes to an entirely different
level where students can for instances practice in the absence of an expert while still having feedback.
Overall, the research underscores the transformative potential of AI in medical education while also
emphasizing the need for careful validationand ongoing evaluation to ensure its effectiveness.

4. Conclusions
The future is already here – it's just not evenly distributed.
(often credit to) William Gibson
Cognitive psychology, artificial intelligence, and computer technology have advanced to
the point where it is feasible to build computer systems that are as effective as
intelligent human tutors.
John Anderson, C. Franklin Boyle and Brain Reiser (1985)
In the paper “Defining the Key Roles and Competencies of the Clinician–Educator of the 21st Century”
published nearly 10 years ago (quote) “Domains of clinician–educator competence endorsed by >85%
of respondents as important or very important were assessment, communication, curriculum
development, education theory, leadership, scholarship, and teaching” (Sherbino et al., 2014).
However, notably absent from these domains were references to artificial intelligence, machine
learning, or even to general informatic-related concepts. A decade has passed, and an entirely new
landscape has emerged, characterized by rapid advancements in technology, particularly in the realm
of healthcare informatics. A shift toward information management, rather than mere acquisition, should
become the new norm. Effective communication skills and empathy remain crucial complements to
these technical competencies (Lee et al., 2021; Wartman and Combs, 2019).
AI has the potential to revolutionize all areas of human intervention. Medical education is no exception.
AI can offer tools and techniques that can greatly enhance training for future healthcare professionals.
Among those personalized learning experiences, immersive simulations and adaptive algorithms are
just a few examples. Alongside these opportunities, there are risks that are inherent to the nature of AI
and therefore not exclusive of medical education. These include ethical concerns such as bias,
overreliance on technology and privacy breaches. Addressing these risks is crucial to ensuring the
responsible integration of AI into medical education. Increased knowledge on AI is one way of
increasing awareness and mitigating potential risks. This involves not only utilizing AI tools within
educational frameworks but also incorporating AI as a topic within the curricula. As AI increasingly
22

permeates educational and clinical workflows, a thorough understanding of its capabilities and
limitations becomes indispensable for all stakeholders, students, healthcare providers and services.
It may seem like an entire decade has passed, but ChatGPT, possibly the most widely used chatbot,
has been available for just over a year (since November 2022). While predicting its long-term impact is
challenging, the immediate potential for its application in medical education appears immense
(Boscardin et al., 2024; Rampton et al., 2020). However, it is essential to recognize that ChatGPT is
just one among many AI tools. In the context of this dissertation, literature on AI applications in medical
education was reviewed, revealing a relatively limited and inconsistent use of AI-based approaches. The
reasons for this limitation, whether stemming from novelty, unpreparedness or potential implementation
costs, remains unclear. While models like ChatGPT exemplify AI's potential, there are still considerable
unexploited opportunities. Several authors have made recommendations in this regard—see, for
instance, (Jowsey et al., 2023). Following the literature review presented in the dissertation, consistent
implementation of AI in educational programs, along with the routine use of AI tools, is highly
recommended. Faculty alignment on this matter is crucial to the success of the entire process. The
citation opening this section, "Cognitive psychology, artificial intelligence, and computer technology
have advanced to the point where it is feasible to build computer systems that are as effective as
intelligent human tutors" (Anderson et al., 1985), remains as relevant today as it was almost 40 years
ago. AI is not a new concept; rather, it is becoming more evenly distributed. It is our responsibility as
educators to ensure that our students receive their deserved and necessary share of AI-enabled
learning opportunities.
