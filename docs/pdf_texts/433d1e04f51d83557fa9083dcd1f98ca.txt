ABSTRACT
Introduction: With the need of re-think how a medical doctor should be in the future, an increasing
emphasis on reflective, competency-based practice has led to an expansion in the use of portfolios in
health professionals (1,2). The great variety of portfolio used in education make it impossible to make a
general statement about them. Despite their many variations, however, we can divide them in two main
types: the reflective and the comprehensive portfolio (3, 4). Still, both types aim to support the learners
learning process and to assess the student’s progress. Methods: At the UMinho Medical School, in
2021 we implemented the use of the portfolio in the 1st and 2nd year of the curriculum where students
have training in Basic Sciences. A portfolio containing 3 components was implemented: 1) Pedagogical
Indicators, 2) Extracurricular activities relevant to professional development and 3) Reflective
Component. It was intended that the student would be able to monitor their evolution, in a critical and
reflective way, with the ultimate objective of promoting a proactive attitude in individual learning. We
analyzed the grades obtained in the portfolio by 1st and 2nd year students, using the results of the 21-22
and 22-23 cohort. Results: We observed that there is no difference in component 2 grades between 1st
and 2nd year learners from the first cohort, but in the second we observed that 2nd year learners had
better grades. We also saw that 2nd year learners, in both cohorts, performed better in component 3. We
showed that, although components 2 and 3 were recognized as relevant, component 1 was not useful
in the sense that, it is a record of classifications obtained in the Knowledge Component and in the Skills
and Attitudes Component and was not even considered for the final score of the portfolio. Conclusion:
As this is a basic science portfolio, we propose to change component 1 and introduce, for laboratory
classes, an e-book where students, for each class, record the objective; methodologies used to achieve
this objective; results; brief discussion about how the methodology was useful in achieving the objective
and a brief reflection on what they learned and how they felt when executing that technical
skill/methodology. We also intend to have activities such as preparation and submission of scientific
posters, journal clubs, letters to editors and discussing cases involving issues related to scientific
integrity and ethics. In the future, the impact of introducing this new portfolio format on the student's
learning and development of a critical and proactive attitude should also be addressed.
Keywords: Basic science; Medical curricula; Medical education; Portfolio.

Introdução: A necessidade de repensar o médico no futuro, levou a uma expansão no uso de portfólios
na educação de profissionais de saúde (1,2). Devido à grande variedade de tipos de portfólio torna
impossível fazer uma observação geral sobre eles. No entanto, e apesar das suas muitas variações,
podemos dividi-los em dois tipos principais: o portfólio reflexivo e portfólios mais abrangentes (3,4).
Ambos os tipos visam apoiar o processo de aprendizagem dos alunos e avaliar o seu progresso.
Métodos: Na Escola Médica da UMinho implementámos em 2021, a utilização do portefólio no 1º e 2º
ano do currículo onde os alunos estão a ter formação em Ciências Básicas. Foi implementado um
portfólio que contem 3 componentes: 1) Indicadores Pedagógicos, 2) Atividades extracurriculares
relevantes para o desenvolvimento profissional e 3) Componente Reflexiva. Pretendeu-se que o aluno
fosse capaz de acompanhar a sua evolução, de forma crítica e reflexiva, com o objetivo final de
promover uma atitude proativa na aprendizagem individual. Analisamos as notas obtidas no portfólio,
pelos alunos do 1º e do 2º ano, usando para isso os resultados da coorte de 21-22 e 22-23.
Resultados: Observamos que não há diferença nas notas da componente 2 entre os alunos do 1º e do
2º ano na primeira coorte, mas na segunda observamos que os alunos do 2º ano tinham melhores
notas. Vimos ainda que os alunos do 2º ano, em ambas as coortes tinham melhor desempenho na
componente 3. Mostramos que, embora as componentes 2 e 3 tenham sido reconhecidas como
relevantes, a componente 1 não foi útil no sentido de que é um registro das classificações obtidas na
Componente de conhecimentos (avaliações sumativas) e na Componente de Competências e Atitudes.
Conclusão: Sendo este um portfólio de ciência básica, propomos alterar a componente 1 e introduzir,
para as aulas laboratoriais, um e-book onde os alunos, para cada aula, registram o objetivo da aula;
metodologias usadas para atingir esse objetivo; os resultados; breve discussão sobre como a
metodologia foi útil para atingir o objetivo e uma breve reflexão sobre o que aprenderam e como se
sentiram na execução daquela habilidade técnica/metodologia. Pretendemos ainda ter atividades como
preparação e submissão de pósteres científicos, jornal clubs, redação de cartas a editores e discussão
de casos que envolvam questões relacionadas à integridade e ética científica. No futuro deverá também
ser abordado o impacto da introdução deste novo formato de portfólio na aprendizagem e
desenvolvimento de uma atitude critica e proativa do aluno.
Palavras-chave: Currículo médico; Educação médica; ensino de ciências básicas; portfólio.

1. INTRODUCTION
In 1990, in a pivotal work in healthcare professions education, Miller described the challenges of
assessing clinical skills/competence/performance. He listed which instruments were available for
assessing those dimensions but clearly stated that no tools were available for assessment of what a
learner does when functioning independently at the clinical workplace (5). Since then, portfolios have
trying to fill this gap and found their way into the field, not only as tools for assessment of performance
in the workplace, but also as tools to stimulate learning from experience. Portfolios are advantageous
for evaluation because they can test areas difficult to assess such as professionalism, continuous
professional development, attitudes and critical thinking.
1.1 Definition of a portfolio and their goals
Traditionally, portfolios were highly used in areas such as architecture and arts. Indeed, portfolios
began to be employed in pedagogy in 1980 and were initially used by architects, photographers,
designers, among others, to show their work. In 1991, Paulson (6) described the portfolio as a
collection of students’ works that reflects the progress, effort, and achievements of an individual.
However, in recent years, the use of portfolios as learning and assessment tools has become more
widespread across the range of other activities namely in health professions (7).
Portfolio, used in education, can be defined as a collection of documentation of performed learning
activities, feedback, and progress (8). The portfolio must have a clear purpose and be aligned with the
learning outcomes of the program. To be successfully implemented a portfolio must be an integral part
of the education with defined tasks for both the learners and the mentors. Support to learners and
mentors support in how to use the portfolio is essential especially in the beginning of the program
(9). Portfolios clearly have a strong formative component that should be fostered and supported by
feedback but, also to be effective it should have a summative character. Indeed, in medical education,
portfolios serve 3 main goals: assessment, reflection and learner development.
Thus, all these concepts are relevant when we are defining a portfolio as it is also illustrated in Figure 1.
2

Figure 1. Universe of the portfolio. Concepts and words that helped on the definition of portfolios.
Portfolios are instruments with multipurpose. Although not initially thought for that, portfolios are
instruments for assessment. Different types of evidence can be combined in portfolios, such as
materials produced by learners (log books, case reports, clinical data), reports of direct observation of
practical procedure but also the reflective practices that should be part of the portfolios. Portfolios are
also instruments for learning. Portfolio learning was a term that was introduced in 1998 (10) and it is
defined as a method of encouraging adult and reflective learning for professionals. It was also
emphasized the importance of supervision and critical reflection for portfolio learning. Indeed, a
portfolio can also stimulate reflection, because, collecting and selecting work samples, evaluations and
other types of materials that illustrate the work that was performed induces learners to look back on
what they have done and analyze what they have achieve and what they should accomplish. Because
portfolios are generally used in a long period of time, they can also be used to support planning and
monitoring in professional development. To stimulate all this process, it should be included learning
objectives in the portfolio as well as a document path of related learning activities and accomplishments
(11, 12). As a consequence, reflections and overviews of personal development have secured a
prominent place in many portfolios. To help in determining whether a portfolio is appropriate for its
intended purpose, the triangle in Figure 2 helps to define the nature of a portfolio. However, a portfolio
can be used to achieve more than one purpose. Indeed, in practice, the majority of portfolios are not
situated in one of the corners of the triangle (13) which is especially relevant to decrease for example
the negative impact that the assessment can have in the student’s hesitancy to expose their own
difficulties on the learning process and in their personal development. On the other hand, if portfolios
3

are not assessed or used for assessment do not reward learners for the time and energy, they invest in
them which leads them to likely take the portfolio and any associated learning activities with less
commitment.
Figure 2. Purposes and content of portfolios (Van Tartwijk et al., 2009) (14).
1.2 Types of portfolios
Portfolios were initially used in medical education for formative purposes in order to encourage
reflection (15). More recently, the use of portfolios has been advocated for summative purposes. The
summative portfolio is used to assess the final learning product of the student and formative portfolio
can guide the student through the learning process with various evaluations.
Also, depending on their purpose, portfolios may differ significantly in scope, structure and content (16).
Thus, some will be little more than a log book recording special activities, while others offer an in-depth
and long-term perspective on professional development over an extended period. Portfolios will normally
be integrally related to a personal or professional learning plan. They may be digital or in paper-based.
The content and the format may be prescribed (closed portfolio) or some level of freedom can be given
to the learner (open portfolio) (9). Although the closed portfolio brings advantages namely for the tutor
to assess different learners, the open portfolio can provide richer descriptions of the learner individual
learning processes and where they can pay attention to specific characteristics of the workplaces (9). It
4

can report on work done, feedback received, progress made, plans for improving competence, and
reflections on performance and development (9). Also, as referred before other different classification is
the distinguish between the reflexive and comprehensive portfolio. While some portfolios may be
developed in order to demonstrate the process of progression, others will be assessed against special
targets of achievement. However, in all cases, the portfolio remains the practical and intellectual
property of the person who develops it. In order to maximize the learning potential of portfolio
development, the learner therefore has to take responsibility for its creation, maintenance and
appropriateness for purpose (16). Also, relevant is the fact that whatever the guiding purpose behind
the portfolio, it should be clear to both the learner and the person making judgements about the
portfolio.
1.3 When to use a portfolio
Portfolios are widely used in undergraduate Healthcare Professions Education but often only in later
clinical years/senior years. It was demonstrated in 2019 (17) that the use of portfolio is well accepted
in the first year of Medical Curriculum and that thorough introduction of the portfolio planned in the
early stages of their studies seems to be the way to train medical learners in reflection. In this study,
the voices of learners n=14 (2 focus groups) and tutors n=6 (semi-structured interviews) who had used
the Undergraduate Medical e-Portfolio in its first introductory year was registered. Analysis uncovered
four major themes: Reflective Practice, Support/Training, e-Portfolio functions, and
Feedback/Assessment. This study revealed that the introduction of portfolio learning in first year of the
Medical Education is relevant in promoting reflective practice and for life-long-learning habits (17). Of
relevance regular small group tutor support and feedback were positive factors which means that each
tutor should be responsible for a group of learners (17) and ideally follow that student during the first
and probably second year of medical graduation. In addition, in this study it was shown that the use of
an authentic e-Portfolio based on a format used in postgraduate years enhanced its support among
early curriculum learners. Also, although recognized that tutoring small group is beneficial, it was
recognized that, especially in the first years, there is a need for further tutor training on reflection and
that Institutional support for reflective practice and the role of an e-Portfolio with good curriculum
integration is important (17). Based on this evidence in use of portfolios in the first years of Medical
Education should be fostered and should be designed based on these characteristics. It is relevant to
take student perceptions into account when implementing a competency-based portfolio and learners
would benefit from coaching on how to select meaningful experiences and performance data for
5

documentation in their portfolios (16, 17). Flexibility in portfolio structure and requirements is essential
to ensure optimal fit between learners' experienced competence development and portfolio content.

2. AIMS
As referred before, the portfolios are important instruments for assessing what a learner does when
functioning independently at the clinical workplace (5). And if in the clinical practice the usage of
portfolios is well accepted and established, the implementation and even the format of the portfolio in
the basic years of the medical graduation is still missing.
The teaching of Basic Sciences as well as laboratory practices is fundamental for medical learners and
is an even more important tool for those who want a profile more focused on scientific research. On the
other hand, in the UMinho Medical graduation, one key aspect that since the foundation was recognized
as a pillar is the fact that medical doctors should be fostered to do research. On that sense, in order to
stimulate learning and life-long learners, while promoting the collection and recording of experiences, as
well as feedback, the implementation of a Basic Sciences portfolio is essential.
Thus, in this dissertation we analyzed the impact of the introduction of portfolio on pre-clinical years in a
medical curriculum and based on that assessment we design a proposal for a Basic Science portfolio.
Our aim is to improve portfolio to the level that the students recognized portfolios as a key element on
their ability to select evidence of learning and reflections and to improve their personal and intellectual
developed.
This is to be implemented in 1st and 2nd year learners in specific activities throughout the different
curricular units.
The objectives of this work are:
1. To foster the basic skills in the learner. As, at the School of Medicine of the UMinho, teaching
in the first years is already based on clinical cases, we intend to measure the development of
more basic laboratory skills as learning outcomes;
2. We also want learners to have a reference document, not only for preparing for the Objective
Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE) of the Curricular Unit of Fundamentals in Medicine
(FM)/ Foundations of medicine (FoM) but also, for those who want to do research, of protocols
for basic techniques that they can use in the future on their PhDs.
8

3. To reformulate existing classes (rather than to introduce new classes), and to implement a
portfolio where learners will have the opportunity to record classes (Log Book) and reflect on
their future in science. Furthermore, it will be a tool where extracurricular activities linked to
scientific activities will be valued.
These objectives were performed and resulted in the work that will be presented, in this thesis, in
the form of an article.

3.1 Abstract
At the School of Medicine of the University of Minho (UMinho), in the medical curriculum, learners at
the end of the first three curricular years obtained a degree in Basic Sciences in Medicine and at the
end of the six curricular years they obtain a Master's degree, being qualified to access all the medical
specialties available in the European space. The teaching of Basic Sciences as well as laboratory
practices are essential for learners that aim to follow a profile focused on scientific research. In that
sense, in order to stimulate learning, while promoting the collection and recording of experiences, as
well as feedback, the implementation of a portfolio in Basic Sciences was critical. For that reason, since
2021 the portfolio in the Basic Sciences was introduced. However, the way it was structured failed in
several aspects namely in: i) giving greater visibility to what has been learned in the student training; ii)
be a document that can be used for consultation in the future and iii) promote the achievement of
autonomy by the student in a research laboratory setting. The reason why this was happening relies on
the structure that was suggested for the portfolio, that do not include, as an example, a register of
activities and laboratory practical skills that were performed during the scholar year in the laboratory
classes, and also because learners were only requested to fill the portfolio at the end of the year instead
of being filled during the entire scholar year in a continuous manner. For that reason, we intend to
reformulate and implement a new structure portfolio of Basic Sciences where the student selects the
evidence of learning and includes reflections on the process developed.
Keywords: Portfolio, Basic Sciences, Medical education, stimulation of scientific profile
12

3.2 Introduction
Portfolios are widely used in Medical education, not only as a source of information for assessment but
also to help learners reflect on their experiences (18). Because portfolios can differ in their purpose,
components and processes, we cannot force that one element, such as reflection, is essential. To
increase our understanding of portfolios, we must recognize that there are different purposes in the
portfolios and also depending of that purpose a variety of components that can contribute to any
particular portfolio model should be considered. All portfolios must have a purpose, which is why
Mathers et al. (19) defined a portfolio as a collection of evidence maintained and presented for a
specific purpose. In Medical Education, van der Vleuten et al. have discussed three functions for a
portfolio: personal development, assessment and learning. And, if for personal development and for
learning, reflection is an essential part in a portfolio, it is not likely to be a relevant component for a
portfolio model designed uniquely for summative assessment. But, because in Medical Education the
majority of the portfolios, were designed for learning purposes there is no doubt that reflection has been
a key component. This led us to the concept that the structure and the components of a portfolio must
accomplish the function that we previously established for a portfolio. Also, for portfolios to be effective
in supporting and assessing competence development, robust integration into the curriculum and tutor
support are essential (20). Importantly previous studies have shown that learners can be quite negative
about portfolio learning and assessment (18) and also because of that, medical educators should
consider introducing portfolios as a method of formative and summative assessment earlier in the
curriculum (21). Motivated by this idea we, in the School of Medicine of the UMinho, introduced in
2021, in the first- and second-year of the curriculum the Portfolio in the Basic Sciences. In this portfolio,
that we initially established as a learning portfolio, we propose the existence of 3 components: 1)
Pedagogical Indicators, 2) Assignments/Deliverables/Extracurricular activities considered as relevant
for the professional development and 3) Reflective Component (Annex 1). With this portfolio, it was
intended that the students monitor their evolution, in a critical and reflective way, with the ultimate
objective of promoting a proactive attitude in each student's individual learning, essential for the
creation of life-long learners.
Overall, we observed that although the implementation of this portfolio stimulated the student’s
participation in extra-curricular activities, but the information that we obtain from them points to the
conclusion that the portfolio stimulus to the student’s learning process was mainly observed in the
reflective component. Moreover, to assess this portfolio, as it was implemented, component 1 which
consists only in a table where the learners need to register the classifications that they obtained in the
13

progress tests and in summative knowledge exams; as no classification was attributed to this
component the information is not relevant for assessment purposes and does not seem to be
stimulating any relevant purpose. As so, there is a need for reappraisal, and changes, to this
component of the portfolio.
Given the implementation of a new structure for the portfolio in the Basic Sciences is needed, we start
to think on the main objectives we aim with this instrument:
-Allow the student to have an organized collection of activities that can serve as a long-term reference –
longitudinal nature.
-Allow the student to monitor their development, know the potential and the aspects that need to be
improved – diversity of content.
-Value the students work by the appreciation and recognition of the effort of the student.
-Use the portfolio as a learning monitoring tool – reflective character.
In brief, in this work we performed an analysis regarding different parameters collected from the
portfolios that were submitted by the learners of the scholar year of 2021/2022 and 2022/2023.
Based on the results obtained, we also propose a new portfolio format to be implemented in the
MinhoMD curriculum at the School of Medicine (UMinho).

3.3 Methods
Context
The portfolio in Basic Science was implemented, in 2020/2021 scholar year, at the School of Medicine
(UMinho, Braga, Portugal), enrolling first and second-year medical students. The portfolio was
introduced in the context of Curricular Unit of Academic Profile (PA) -1 for the students of the 1-years
and PA-2 for the students of the 2-year. It is a mandatory activity as it is used for summative evaluation
of that Curricular Unit.
Portfolio structure
The Portfolio is divided into 3 components: 1) Pedagogical Indicators, 2) Tasks/Deliverables and 3)
Reflective Component. With this portfolio, it is intended that the student monitors their evolution, in a
critical and reflective way, with the ultimate objective of promoting a proactive attitude in each student's
individual learning, essential for creating lifelong learners.
In component 1 two aspects were considered: 1) Knowledge Component – where the learners need to
fill a table with only the final classification obtained in the summative assessments and 2) Skills and
Attitudes Component – where the learners need to fill with the final classification that they obtained in
the OSCE exam (skills) and, the final score of the attitudes that were obtained by the mean of the
classifications that were attributed by the teacher in the different classes.
In component 2 the learners are invited to describe any extra-curricular tasks/assignments developed
during the curricular year with impact on CanMeds competence framework for professional
development (Medical Expert including: Communicator, Collaborator, Leader, Health Advocate, Scholar,
Professional). For this the learners described which was the activity that they participated, classification
(if applicable) and the relevance of that activity for the professional future.
In component 3 learners need to reflect on their academic and professional future
(Leader/Communicator). As suggestion the learners are required to reflect on possible topics to cover:
“What do I need to improve in each section”, “what do I need to maintain”, “what is the plan for
continuous improvement?”, “what are the main challenges, difficulties and successes”, “how do
challenges and successes contribute to learning about oneself and the context in which one is
inserted”).
15

Students know in advance which are the components that they will need to fill to deliver the portfolio.
Portfolio assessment
The portfolio was assessed at the end of each scholar year and each teacher was responsible for the
assessment of 10-12 portfolios. As referred before, component 1 was not assessed because it is a
register of the classifications obtained by the student in the knowledge and skill exams and
professionalism obtained in the different classes. This quantitative information is useful for the learned
then to reflect in the component 3. Then, component 2 is assessed in a scale 0-3 in which the quote
must consider: a) whether they are actually extracurricular activities and not activities of some curricular
unit; b) the relevance of the activities; c) the duration of the activities. Component 3 is assessed in a
scale of 0-7 taking into account: a) correctness of writing; b) ability to raise pertinent questions; c)
maturity in the proposed response to the questions raised; d) suggestion of personal changes for the
next academic year.
Statistical analysis
Data that was analyzed in this work was obtained from the portfolio of students in the first and second
year of the Medical degree from the University of Minho (years of 2021/2022 and 2022/2023). A
confidence interval of 95% was assumed for all statistical tests. Regarding continuous variables, the
assumption of normality was assessed by qualitative analysis of Q-Q plots and frequency distributions,
the z-score of skewness and kurtosis, as well as by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests.
Mann-Whitney U-test was applied for all the data presented, except when reported otherwise.
Correlations were carried out using Spearman’s correlation coefficient (rS). The sample size was as
follows: N=146 and 126 (Year 2021/2022, PA1 and PA2, respectively); N= 157 and 129 (Year
2022/2023, PA1 and PA2, respectively). Data is presented as median (min-max) and plotted using
GraphPad prism 8.

This data is highly relevant and it highlight the relevance of having a completely different strategy to
assess portfolios. Of relevance until the moment, we cannot assume that we have a mentoring program
to guide our learners in the portfolios. But it is described that the system works well when it operates
through the interaction of a learner and mentor using the material as a catalyst to guide further
learning. So, in the future it will be essential to create a mentoring strategy during the portfolio
construction and not to have an evaluator that will assess the portfolio only at the end of the scholar
year. Regarding this aspect it is essential that the mentoring support should be based on the principle
that the support is non-judgmental (16). Its purpose is to enable the learner to develop a portfolio that
meets his/her needs and explore means to achieve this. The mentor may offer opinions and add issues
based on personal experience, but should not take responsibility for the eventual actions of the learner
in developing the portfolio (16). Other important aspect, that should be taken into consideration is that
mentoring is a totally separate activity from either appraising or assessing the learner (16).
Then, to better understand if the portfolio, in the way it is organized is contributing for the learners to

This lack of correlation is not completely surprising in the sense that none of the components of the

which they are required to recall in the OSCEs. This reinforces the idea that our portfolio and its

Consequently, and if we want the portfolio to have an impact in the proficiency of the practical skills of
the learners, we may replace the component 1, which is not being used to assess the portfolio, for a
component that will bring a learning component for the learners. Being this a portfolio in basic science
the suggestion is to have in part 1 an e-lab book where, the learners for each practical class that they
have during the medical curriculum in the first and second year, can register the objective of that class;
which methodologies they needed to follow to reach that objective; which were the results and a brief
discussion on how the methodology was useful in reaching the final objective. Also, they would need to
write a small reflection on what they learnt and how they felt confident in performing that technical
skill/methodology (Figure 10).

3.5 Conclusion
It is well established that portfolios enhance medical knowledge, promote professionalism, and
encourage lifelong learning (23, 24). Successful portfolio programs include structured content
guidelines, meaningful experiences for reflection, summative assessments, and faculty coaching (25).
Due to these characteristics and attending to the data obtained and discussed in this work we
suggested the implementation of a new structure for the portfolio in the basic sciences that will better
allow:
-The creation of a communities of practice that can help both learners and faculty to stimulate the
profile more linked to science in medical education.
-Understand the acquisition of theoretical knowledge and practical skills by learners.
-Stimulate the learners to reflect throughout the entire year on the technical skills learnt.
-Understand the extent to which learners reflect on issues such as scientific ethics; and reflections for
its development with recognition of weaknesses and strengths; self-assessment and self-direction.
For sure that it will be necessary to initially motivate learners to fill in the portfolio and try to create the
idea that it is not a waste of time and that it can be an important tool. On the other hand, it will be
necessary to gather a qualified and available faculty to monitor the learners and final evaluation.
