RESUMO
Escala de Avaliação de Sutura do Minho – Desenvolvimento e Validação
Objetivo: Esta investigação introduz e valida a Escala de Avaliação de Sutura do Minho (Minho-
SAS), uma ferramenta abrangente concebida para a avaliação de competências fundamentais de
sutura em estudantes de medicina. Utilizando a Teoria de Resposta ao Item (IRT) e um formato
dicotômico de resposta sim/não, a escala fornece uma estrutura detalhada para avaliar as
competências de sutura.
Métodos: O processo de desenvolvimento envolveu a colaboração com equipas multidisciplinares
e profissionais experientes para desenvolver a Minho-SAS e garantir a sua validade. Os métodos
de validação incorporaram técnicas de IRT, utilizando o modelo Rasch e o modelo 2-PL para avaliar
a confiabilidade, validade de construto e precisão de medição. Foi realizada uma avaliação
psicométrica rigorosa utilizando dados de uma coorte de estudantes do 5º ano de medicina.
Resultados: A Minho-SAS final consiste em 15 itens dicotômicos sim/não e exibiu uma robusta
consistência interna, confiabilidade e validade de construto nos modelos Rasch e 2-PL. A análise
dos itens revelou perceções sobre o desempenho individual dos itens, elucidando diferentes níveis
de dificuldade e discriminação entre os 15 componentes da avaliação. A ênfase da escala nas
competências fundamentais de sutura foi evidente na sua avaliação detalhada, diferenciando-a de
ferramentas de avaliação mais amplas.
Conclusão: A Minho-SAS revela-se um promissor instrumento especificamente adaptado para a
avaliação de competências fundamentais de sutura em estudantes de medicina. A sua rigorosa
validação através de metodologias de IRT sublinha a sua precisão e fiabilidade, posicionando-a
como uma adição valiosa às escalas de avaliação existentes. No entanto, é essencial uma maior
exploração em diversas populações e ambientes educacionais para confirmar sua aplicabilidade e
impacto.
Palavras-Chave: Avaliação; Competências; Sutura, Teoria de Resposta ao Item, Validação

ABSTRACT
Minho Suture Assessment Scale – Development and Validation
Objective: This research introduces and validates the Minho Suture Assessment Scale (Minho-
SAS), a comprehensive tool designed for the evaluation of fundamental suturing competencies in
medical students. Employing Item Response Theory (IRT) and a dichotomous yes/no response
format, the scale provides a detailed framework for assessing suturing abilities.
Methods: The development process involved collaboration with multidisciplinary teams and
experienced practitioners to develop the Minho-SAS and to ensure content validity. Validation
methods incorporated IRT techniques, utilizing the Rasch model and the 2-PL model to assess
reliability, construct validity, and measurement precision. Rigorous psychometric evaluation was
conducted using data from a cohort of 5th-year medical students.
Results: The final Minho-SAS consisted of 15 dichotomic yes/no items and exhibited robust
internal consistency, reliability, and construct validity across both Rasch and 2-PL models. Item
analyses revealed insights into individual item performance, elucidating varying levels of difficulty
and discrimination among the 15 assessment components. The scale's emphasis on fundamental
suturing skills was evident in its detailed evaluation, setting it apart from broader assessment tools.
Conclusion: The Minho-SAS proves to be a promising instrument specifically tailored for
assessing fundamental suturing skills in medical students. Its rigorous validation through IRT
methodologies underscores its precision and reliability, positioning it as a valuable addition to
existing evaluation scales. While showing considerable promise, further exploration across diverse
populations and educational settings is essential to confirm its broader applicability and impact in
medical education and clinical practice.
Keywords: Assessment; Competencies; Item Response Theory; Suture; Validation.

INTRODUCTION
Suture proficiency is an indispensable skill for young doctors, who are expected to
autonomously treat patients with skin wounds after finishing their training. Despite its crucial
nature, the teaching and assessment of this skill during undergraduate medical training are often
insufficient, posing challenges for medical students in acquiring essential competencies for
immediate post-graduation practice. While medical educators recognize the importance of suturing
skills, there is a notable absence of regular and structured assessments in medical schools, leading
to a deficit in competency acquisition. In the existing literature, available tools for assessing
technical and surgical competencies primarily target more complex procedures, predominantly
applicable to surgical residents rather than undergraduates. Recognizing this gap, the authors
believe there is a need for a fundamental suture skill assessment scale tailored specifically to
medical students. To bridge this void, the authors propose the development and validation of a
new scale utilizing Item Response Theory (IRT) as an innovative approach to suture scale validation.
The assessment of competency presents challenges, partly due to the difficulty in defining
and isolating the specific competency to be evaluated, given the multifaceted nature of professional
competency dimensions. Various competencies-based curricula, such as CanMEDS (Frank, 2004)
and Accreditation Councill for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) core competencies (Derstine
et al., sem data), have attempted to address this challenge. Competence, as explained by Epstein
et al., is contextual, reflecting the relationship between a person's abilities and the tasks they are
required to perform in real-world situations (Epstein, 2002). In the context of undergraduate
performance assessment, students can be evaluated in simulated or real-life environments,
utilizing various instruments depending on the measured competence (Epstein, 2007).
Surgical skills constitute a vital component of the undergraduate curriculum, with the ability
to perform a basic suture being a fundamental requirement for medical graduates (Emmanuel et
al., 2021). However, recent reports indicate a concerning decline in suture competence among
recent medical graduates (Emmanuel et al., 2021). Existing suture assessment scales, while
available, predominantly focus on specialized areas such as laparoscopic/robotic sutures
(Vassiliou et al., 2005), microsurgery (Almeland et al., 2020; Nugent et al., 2012), or are tailored
to post-graduate surgical residents (Chipman & Schmitz, 2009; Dormegny et al., 2023; Scott et
al., 2007). Some scales aimed at medical students include time to perform as a component, a
controversial factor in the literature, as the expected competency for undergraduates should
prioritize the quality of technique over speed (Sundhagen et al., 2018).
1

Acknowledging the contextual and developmental nature of competence, the authors
believe that assessments must be tailored to the specific context. They claim the need to
differentiate those who are competent from those who are unable to perform. Additionally, the
authors aim to enhance the specificity, detail, and standardization of the assessment tool, reducing
subjectivity and facilitating use by less experienced assessors in summative exams.
Additionally, due to the novelty of assessing suture skills in the Minho School of Medicine,
the authors, who were part of the surgical curricular unit teaching staff, felt the need for a validated
process in the specific context of an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) for 5th- year
medical students.
The assessment of competencies, particularly in complex domains like medical education,
necessitates robust methodologies that provide accurate and reliable measurements. Classical
Test Theory (CTT) and IRT are two distinct frameworks employed in educational measurement.
CTT, a traditional approach, estimates the reliability of a test by calculating the standard error of
measurement for a given group of students. While widely used, CTT has limitations such as being
sample-dependent and assuming constant reliability across all performance levels. In contrast, IRT
offers a more sophisticated and flexible approach. By focusing on the characteristics of individual
test items, IRT estimates a candidate's true ability, incorporating factors like item difficulty and
discrimination. The two commonly used IRT models, the one-parameter logistic (1-PL)/Rasch and
the two-parameter logistic (2-PL), provide valuable insights into the nuances of test performance.
Unlike CTT, IRT is sample-independent, ensuring applicability across various expertise levels and
offering a more precise estimation of measurement error at each point along the ability scale (De
Champlain, 2010). For proper validation and understanding of the measurement process, the
authors advocate for the use of Item Response Theory over classic test theory.
In conclusion, the authors propose to address the identified problem by developing and
validating the Minho Suture Assessment Scale (Minho-SAS), a dichotomic scale using IRT. This
novel approach aims to differentiate competent from non-competent undergraduate medical
students in basic suture skills within a summative context.

METHODS
Part I: Identification of the domains to be assessed.
The authors aimed to develop an evaluation tool capable of discerning undergraduates
proficient in correctly executing a fundamental suture. The instrument was designed to be user-
friendly, in the Portuguese language, and tailored to the context of a summative assessment for
5th-year medical students within a simulated scenario. As far as our knowledge extends, no other
scale serving this specific purpose and context has undergone validation. To establish the best
practices for scale validation, the authors followed the recommendations from Boateng and the
Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (American Educational Research Association
et al., 2014; Boateng et al., 2018).
Part II: Development of the Minho-Suture Assessment Scale
The authors employed a simultaneous application of both inductive and deductive
approaches to generate scale items. A focus group comprised of fourteen skilled surgeons with
diverse backgrounds, including general surgery, pediatric surgery, gynecology, orthopedics, and
urology, was carefully assembled. The group utilized a Delphi methodology to collaboratively
propose a list of items for inclusion in the initial Minho-SAS prototype. Each specialist contributed
with suggestions and feedback. During the primary round, items were deliberated upon, duplicates
were removed, wording was standardized, and a consensus was reached. The final draft,
containing 15 yes/no items and a global performance scale with a Likert-type rating ranging from
1 to 5 (Table 1), underwent thorough revision and received approval from the group.
Part III: Scale Testing and Sample Size
Adhering to the 10:1 rule of thumb for sample size determination (Goodhue et al., 2012),
implying a minimum of 150 assessments, the authors employed the Minho-SAS to assess the
performance of 269 fifth-year medical students participating in OSCE during a surgical clerkship,
corresponding to two consecutive yearly OSCE. The evaluations were conducted by six highly
experienced medical doctors, trained in the technique, and possessing ample assessment
experience, reducing the risk of bias.
4

Part IV: Statistical Analysis
To validate our assumptions regarding the Minho-SAS results, we assert that the test score
serves as a reliable approximation of the true ability (suturing ability), focusing solely on one
competency without undue influence from other variables. The belief is that scale takers with higher
grades would exhibit greater success in an advanced course compared to students with lower
grades. To examine this unidimensionality, a key assumption to conduct an IRT analysis, a Q3
correlation matrix was performed to analyze residuals (differences between observed and predicted
values), and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to explore the underlying structure
of the dataset.
Additionally, the authors incorporated multiple sources of validity evidence:
A. Test Content: Content validity was ensured by obtaining approval from a focus group, as
previously described. The scale was further endorsed by expert medical professionals with
experience in suture and psychometrics, reinforcing alignment with the intended content.
B. Response Processes: To ensure the fit between the assessment process and the construct,
assessors were questioned about their thought processes, specifically regarding the
evaluation of suture skills and whether they perceived they were assessing other
competencies. Surgical experts accompanied assessors during the initial assessments to
enhance the alignment between the assessment process and the construct.
C. Internal Structure: To conduct this analysis, the authors employed IRT, which is a statistical
and psychometric framework that models the relationship between the individual’s latent
trait (an unobservable trait, in this case, suture ability) and the result of Minho-SAS items.
Contrary to CTT, IRT focuses on the item’s properties and its ability to discriminate
between individuals with different skill levels. Within the IRT analysis, the authors applied
two models: Rasch model and the 2-parameter logistic model (2-PL). The Rasch model is
a one-parameter logistic model that assumes that the probability of responding correctly
to an item (or having that item classified as yes/obtained) is dependent on the responder's
ability (Theta – θ) and the item’s difficulty (b). The 2-PL model extends the Rasch, by
introducing another component: the item’s discrimination (a) that reflects how well an item
discriminates students with higher and lower ability levels. To assess internal consistency,
the authors used Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald's omega, ensuring reliability estimates
of the scale items (Baker, 2001; Boateng et al., 2018; Nima et al., 2020; YANG & KAO,
2014; Zanon et al., 2016).
5

D. Relations with Other Variables: The Minho-SAS was validated in conjunction with the
established technical assessment scale OSATS (Martin et al., 1997), assuring the accuracy
of the results. A correlation analysis and Cohen's d were conducted between the test
scores of the two assessment tools to enhance the overall validity of the findings.
E. Validity of Consequences of Testing: Faculty members responsible for the curricular unit
blueprint and mapping can modify the curriculum based on the analysis of test scores,
addressing potential gaps in student learning. The future application of the scale for
feedback delivery is also contemplated, responding to one of the most common student
requests: more feedback.
This comprehensive approach aims to thoroughly validate the Minho-SAS, providing a
robust foundation for its application in assessing suture skills among fifth-year medical students.
For the analysis software, the authors used R version 4.3.1 with the mirt package, JASP
((JASP Team (2023). JASP (Version 0.18.1) [Computer software].) and jamovi (v2.3.28), retrieved
from https://www.jamovi.org.

In Rasch analysis, item fit statistics presented in Table 6 serve as indicators of how well
the data align with the model. Outfit is a measure of how closely an item corresponds to the
anticipated pattern of responses outlined by the Rasch model. An Outfit value that is excessively
high or low may suggest that the item is either too challenging or too easy compared to
12

expectations. Distinctively, Infit assesses response consistency and is more sensible to patterns of
responses. Deviations in Infit values, whether too high or too low, may signal potential confusion
or misalignment of an item with others in the scale. Fit values ideally fall within the range of 0.5 to
1.5, and items with Infit or Outfit exceeding 2.0 are typically considered for exclusion (Cecilio-
Fernandes et al., 2017; Dichotomous Mean-Square Fit Statistics, sem data; What do Infit and
Outfit, Mean-square and Standardized mean?, sem data).
In this work, the observed Outfit range from 0.479 to 1.253, indicating an acceptable
variability in the behavior of the items. Similarly, the Infit statistics range from 0.954 to 1.247,
suggesting good levels of response consistency. The mean values for Outfit (0.782) and Infit
(0.971) signify an overall alignment of items with the Rasch model, although with some variability
in their fit.

DISCUSSION
This work addresses the creation and validation of a suture assessment scale tailored for
5th-year medical students – Minho-SAS. As we delve into the discussion, a synthesis of our
methodologies and key findings becomes paramount. The exploration of the Minho-SAS’s internal
consistency, reliability coefficients derived from IRT, using the Rasch and 2-PL models, and model
fit analysis lays the foundation for a nuanced understanding of the assessment scale's efficacy.
This discussion aims to unravel the intricacies of our validation processes, shedding light on the
statistical measures employed and the implications of the observed outcomes. The overarching
goal is to unveil the reliability, validity, and practical implications of the Minho-SAS, providing
valuable insights into its potential integration into medical training programs and its role in shaping
the future of suture skills assessment. Through a comprehensive exploration of these aspects, this
discussion will underscore the significance of our study in enhancing the precision and
effectiveness of suturing skill evaluation among 5th-year medical students.
The creation of the Minho-SAS involved a collaborative process to ensure the development
of a robust and contextually relevant suture assessment scale. Employing both inductive and
deductive approaches simultaneously, fourteen skilled surgeons with diverse backgrounds were
selected to form a focus group, respecting the Delphi methodology. This tailored approach to scale
creation ensures that the Minho-SAS encapsulates the diverse facets of suturing skills deemed
critical for 5th-year medical students in simulated scenarios and what will be required in their future
practice.
In terms of validation methodologies, conventional techniques such as exploratory and
confirmatory factor analysis are suboptimal for the Minho-SAS, given its dichotomous nature.
Instead, IRT emerges as a pivotal approach for scale validation, offering distinct advantages. IRT
models the relationship between an individual's latent trait, specifically suture skill in this case, and
their assessments in the individual items within the Minho-SAS. This stands in contrast to classical
test theory, which treats all items as equivalent, not considering item difficulty and discrimination.
In this study, we used two IRT models: the Rasch model and the 2-PL. The Rasch model, a one-
parameter model, assumes unidimensionality, focusing on a single latent trait uninfluenced by
external factors. Each item in this model possesses a difficulty parameter (b), indicating the point
on the ability continuum where individuals have a 50% probability of receiving a positive
assessment. The 2-PL model, an extension of one-parameter models, introduces a discrimination
16

parameter (a), quantifying an item's ability to differentiate between students with varying ability
levels.
In the journey towards validating the Minho-SAS, an in-depth model fit analysis was
conducted, exploring how well the data align with the prescribed models. The utilization of AIC and
SABIC values, outlined in Tables 4 and 5, served as compass points guiding the assessment of
model fit. Lower AIC and SABIC values signify a superior fit, emphasizing the accuracy of the Minho-
SAS. Crucial to our investigation was the confirmation of unidimensionality, a fundamental aspect
for the application of IRT. Residuals analysis and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (Table 2 and
3) were employed to scrutinize the dimensionality of the Minho-SAS. Notably, our data exhibited
no compromise to the assumption of independence, as indicated by correlation coefficient
residuals values <0.3. This unidimensionality was further supported by the observed patterns in
the Principal Component Analysis, revealing a dominant single component with substantial
loadings of variables onto it.
Assessing suture skill proficiency and determining the scale's appropriateness for students
with varying skill levels are crucial aspects of scale refinement. Ideally, the scale's items should
span a range of difficulty levels, enhancing its effectiveness in measuring proficiency. Items with
higher discrimination parameters are particularly valuable as they excel in distinguishing between
high and low performers. In the Rasch model, observed in Table 5, item difficulty parameters
demonstrate a wide distribution, ranging from 0.628 (item 13) to 4.02 (item 3), indicating a
progressive difficulty level spectrum within the Minho-SAS. The 2-PL analysis reaffirms this
distribution, with item difficulty values ranging from 0.573 (item 13) to 5.08 (item 3). An intriguing
shift in item 14's difficulty from Rasch to 2-PL analysis hints at its challenging nature.
Discrimination parameters, ranging from 0.525 (item 1) to 2.935 (item 14), highlight the diverse
effectiveness of items in discerning suture skills and contributing to the Minho-SAS's accuracy
across a spectrum of proficiency levels.
In the Item Characteristic Curve (ICC) analysis, item 3 exhibits a tall and narrow curve,
signifying high precision in measuring abilities, while item 6 displays a broader ICC, implying
information coverage across a wider range of skills. Items characterized by steeper curves,
exemplified by item 3, indicate higher discrimination values, effectively distinguishing students
based on their suture skill levels. Conversely, items with shallower curves, like item 5, possess
lower discrimination values. The peak of the ICC signifies an item's difficulty, with item 2 peaking
at a lower ability level, denoting an easy item, and item 4 peaking at a higher ability level, indicating
17

a more challenging item. For instance, the ICC curve of item 6 suggests a higher probability of
success for students with advanced skill levels, depicting it as a more challenging item. In contrast,
the ICC curve for item 15 implies a less challenging nature.
Regarding the Information Curve (IIC), it is noteworthy that the peak of information is
observed around Θ values of -2 in the Rasch Analysis and Θ=0 in the 2-PL analysis. Information
decreases when moving beyond this range of ability. This implies that, in the Rasch analysis, the
Minho-SAS yields the most informative measure for students with lower suture skill proficiency,
which aligns with the purpose of this assessment and scale creation. Conversely, the 2-PL analysis
captures more detailed information for students with average ability levels.
The scrutiny of item fit and outfit, outlined in Table 6, further elucidates the performance
of the Minho-SAS concerning the Rasch model. Outfit, gauging the overall fit of items, and infit,
sensitive to the consistency of responses, are pivotal components of the assessment. The observed
values of outfit ranging from 0.479 to 1.253 indicate acceptable variability in the behavior of the
items. Similarly, infit statistics ranging from 0.954 to 1.247 suggest commendable levels of
response consistency. It is essential to note that the mean values for both outfit (0.479) and infit
(0.954) signify an overall alignment of items with the Rasch model. While some variability in fit
exists, these findings affirm the general appropriateness of the Minho-SAS in accurately assessing
suturing skills.
The internal consistency, as estimated by McDonald's ω and Cronbach's α values
presented in Table 7, attests to the robustness of the scale. These reliability measures reinforce
our confidence in the Minho-SAS, indicating that the individual items within the scale consistently
contribute to the measurement of the intended construct — an imperative quality for any
assessment tool. Furthermore, the reliability coefficients generated for both the Rasch Model (0.71)
and the 2-PL Model (0.74) fall within an acceptable range for assessment tools. This attests to the
scale's ability to yield consistent results across different ability models, as illustrated in Figures 5
and 6. These figures depict patterns of reliability, showcasing the Minho-SAS's efficacy in reliably
assessing suturing skills, regardless of the underlying proficiency levels of the individuals being
evaluated. In essence, the validation process not only establishes the internal consistency of the
Minho-SAS but also emphasizes its reliability across different models, which is of paramount
importance for ensuring that the scale remains a robust and dependable tool for evaluating suturing
skills.

The correlation analysis between latent traits (theta) and the aggregated Minho-SAS scores,
detailed in Table 8, unveils a close relationship. The exceptionally high correlation levels, notably
0.96 from the 2-PL model and 0.982 from the Rasch model, underscore the scale's effectiveness
in capturing and reflecting students' abilities accurately. This robust correlation signifies that the
Minho-SAS not only aligns with the theoretical constructs posited by the Rasch model and 2-PL
model but also provides a reliable representation of students' suturing abilities. The findings
suggest that the Minho-SAS serves as a valid and dependable tool for evaluating the suturing skills
of medical students, offering valuable insights into their aptitude in this critical aspect of medical
practice.
To perceive any discernible differences between the two assessed years, an independent
samples t-test and Cohen’s d coefficient were employed and are delineated in Table 9. The results
shed light on potential variations in suturing proficiency among 5th-year medical students across
different academic years. The independent samples t-test examines mean score differences,
providing insights into whether there are statistically significant variations in the performance of
students from different cohorts. Additionally, the utilization of Cohen’s d coefficient allows for an
exploration of the effect size, offering a quantitative measure of the magnitude of any observed
differences. By scrutinizing the comparative data, we can gain a nuanced understanding of how
the Minho-SAS performs across different academic years, providing valuable implications for the
ongoing refinement of medical education practices. Any identified differences may inform targeted
interventions or enhancements in the curriculum, contributing to a more tailored and effective
approach to teaching and assessing suturing skills.
Undoubtedly, the study possesses noteworthy strengths. The integration of IRT principles,
particularly the Rasch and 2-PL models, ensures a rigorous and theoretically grounded approach
to scale development. The robust internal consistency, reliability coefficients, and model fit analysis
affirm the Minho-SAS's suitability for evaluating suturing skills among 5th-year medical students.
However, acknowledging limitations is crucial for a balanced perspective. Potential biases in
participant selection, the generalizability of findings to broader medical student populations, and
the contextual constraints of simulated scenarios are inherent limitations. These considerations
underscore the necessity for caution in extrapolating the results to real-world clinical contexts.
Despite these limitations, the study's strengths solidify the Minho-SAS as a valuable tool in suture
skills assessment, contributing meaningfully to the broader discourse on medical education
evaluation methodologies.

has tangible implications for curriculum design and the integration of targeted interventions to
enhance suturing proficiency. Recommendations stem from our findings, advocating for the
incorporation of the Minho-SAS into medical training programs. This would not only provide a
standardized and reliable method for assessing suturing skills but also contribute to the ongoing
refinement of curricula. The scale's potential role in identifying specific areas of improvement for
individual students further enhances its utility. As the medical education landscape evolves, our
study encourages educators and institutions to consider the Minho-SAS as a strategic component
in shaping the next generation of adept medical practitioners.

CONCLUSION
Our investigation extensively scrutinized the internal consistency, precision, and
multifaceted performance of the Minho-SAS, affirming its potential as a reliable and accurate
assessment tool. Filling a critical gap in evaluating basic suture skills among medical students, the
Minho-SAS introduces a standardized and objective approach to proficiency assessment. The
integration of IRT within the validation process enhances its standing as a precise instrument for
evaluating suture skills by offering insightful information about each item’s performance. Despite
the robust validation, certain limitations necessitate acknowledgment. Relying on a specific cohort
and a limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of findings, cautioning against
extrapolating results beyond the study's context. While acknowledging these limitations, the study
highlights the Minho-SAS's potential versatility across diverse contexts. Looking ahead, further
research, particularly through multicentric studies and larger, more diverse samples, can
strengthen its validation and universality. Despite limitations, this study establishes a foundation
for future endeavors, offering promise in refining and expanding the Minho-SAS's utility in diverse
educational and clinical settings. This validated tool stands poised to enhance medical education,
providing an objective means of evaluating and enhancing suture skills, ultimately contributing to
the overall competence and proficiency of future healthcare professionals.
